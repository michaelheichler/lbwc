# Chapter 08: Before the Project

> Requirements don't exist until you uncover them through feedback loops with clients — no process or toolbox replaces thinking and direct collaboration.

## Tips in this chapter

- **Tip 75 — "No One Knows Exactly What They Want"** — The initial statement of need is an invitation to explore, not a specification to implement.
- **Tip 76 — "Programmers Help People Understand What They Want"** — Our most valuable skill is surfacing what clients actually need, not just building what they said.
- **Tip 77 — "Requirements Are Learned in a Feedback Loop"** — Generate feedback (mockups, prototypes, working code) and let clients refine their thinking through it.
- **Tip 78 — "Work with a User to Think Like a User"** — Sit with users while they do their jobs; domain immersion reveals real requirements that interviews miss.
- **Tip 79 — "Policy Is Metadata"** — Implement the general case; encode the specific policy as data or configuration so it can change without code changes.
- **Tip 80 — "Use a Project Glossary"** — One place that defines every domain term; shared by all participants to eliminate naming ambiguity.
- **Tip 81 — "Don't Think Outside the Box — Find the Box"** — Identify and challenge every assumed constraint; distinguish real limits from preconceived ones before deciding what's impossible.
- **Tip 82 — "Don't Go into the Code Alone"** — Pair or mob programming reduces bad habits, surfaces issues earlier, and distributes knowledge.
- **Tip 83 — "Agile Is Not a Noun; Agile Is How You Do Things"** — Agility is a style of responding to change, not a boxed process to install.

## Ponytail relevance

### Requirements are not architecture — don't over-specify early (SUPPORTS)
The book draws a hard line: "Requirements are not architecture. Requirements are not design, nor are they the user interface. Requirements are need." Over-specified requirements documents are wrong twice — the client doesn't read them, and they lock in decisions that haven't been earned yet. The laziest correct approach is the index-card user story that keeps scope small and forces clarifying conversations before the code is written.

### Policy Is Metadata (SUPPORTS)
Tip 79 is a direct laziness win: instead of hard-coding business rules ("only supervisors can view employee records"), implement access control as configurable data. The general mechanism is written once; the specific policy is just a record in a table. This is the minimum code that survives the inevitable policy change.

### The smallest meaningful step — then evaluate (SUPPORTS)
The agile recipe (locate where you are → make the smallest meaningful step → evaluate and fix) aligns exactly with ponytail's minimalism. The book shows how applying the loop even at variable-naming level prevents over-engineering: renaming `user` to `owner` leads to the question "do I actually need the whole account owner, or just an email?" — and the answer eliminates a coupling. Build only what the next step requires.

### Don't Think Outside the Box — Find the Box (SUPPORTS)
Tip 81 is a constraint audit. Before deciding a problem requires a complex solution, enumerate every assumed constraint and prove each one is real. Many "impossible" problems are only hard because of constraints that don't actually exist. The laziest path is the one that dissolves the problem, not the one that heroically solves it.

### Feedback loop kills requirements creep (GUARDRAIL)
Short iterations with direct client feedback are the mechanism for catching scope drift early. "If you're working with the client in iterations with constant feedback, then the client will experience first-hand the impact of 'just one more feature.'" The discipline of the feedback loop is the not-careless half: skipping it lets scope silently inflate until the project is unsalvageable.

### Project Glossary — naming is a trust boundary (GUARDRAIL)
Tip 80 is not optional housekeeping. Using the wrong word for a domain concept ("client" vs "customer" in a system that distinguishes them) is a correctness defect, not a style issue. The glossary costs little to maintain and prevents entire categories of bugs and miscommunication.

### Working Together — pair/mob programming (TENSION)
Tip 82 and the pair/mob programming section push toward higher upfront coordination cost. Solo work is faster to start; pairing costs two people's time. The tension is real. The book's justification: "the inherent peer-pressure of a second person helps against moments of weakness" — naming variables `foo`, taking embarrassing shortcuts. Worth it for non-trivial problems; overkill for one-liners.

## Sharpest lines

- "The best requirements documentation, perhaps the only requirements documentation, is working code."
- "Requirements are not architecture. Requirements are not design, nor are they the user interface. Requirements are need."
- "Whenever someone says 'do this, and you'll be agile,' they are wrong. By definition."
- "Some of our most successful work has happened when we started working on a client's requirements, took a single step, and realized that what we were about to do wasn't necessary, that the best solution didn't even involve software."
