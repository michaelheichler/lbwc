# Evangelism Methods

This chapter gives you four concrete methods for building organizational awareness and support for UX -- not by broadcasting at colleagues, but by opening conversations, building relationships, and helping others see how UX work serves their own goals. Use these methods when you are the only UX practitioner and need to grow a base of supporters who will sponsor and multiply your work.

---

## Method 22: Captive UX

### What it is and when to use it

Ambient, low-effort promotion placed where people already are: elevator interiors, restroom doors, break rooms, hallways, meeting rooms. Creates repeated casual exposure to UX work and results. Use when you want to build awareness quickly and with minimal effort, or after completing work worth showcasing.

*Average time: one to two hours to design and post flyers.*

### How to do it

1. **Define your message.** Choose one angle: (a) a project newsletter showing outcomes and how they benefited a department; (b) an awareness piece about UX resources available to others (which may be you); or (c) a direct connection between your UX work and business KPIs -- helping colleagues on the business side see how your work helps them hit their own targets.
2. **Design for readability.** Create a one-page flyer. Show a draft to a colleague to confirm it casts UX in a clear, positive light. No UX jargon -- speak the language of the people you are addressing.
3. **Include contact information** so interested readers can follow up.
4. **Post and remove.** Distribute copies, then remove them after a week or two.
5. **Repeat every few months.** A novel message in a new location keeps getting attention.

*For remote teams: convert to a periodic email newsletter.*

### Inputs / Outputs

- **Inputs:** One completed UX project with a describable outcome; basic layout tools.
- **Outputs:** Flyer distributed to the office; growing ambient familiarity with UX; inbound inquiries over time.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Permission vs. forgiveness.** Going through approval channels risks being told no and adds friction to a deliberately lightweight method. If the flyers are tasteful and informative, post them.
- **Expect slow results.** The first round will not flood your inbox. The goal is accumulated familiarity -- people grow more receptive to things over time.

## Method 23: Mini Case Studies

### What it is and when to use it

A one-page summary of a completed UX project that leads with impact and is legible to non-UX colleagues. "Mini" is a constraint: short stories are easier to remember, share, and tell. Do one after every project or major piece of work. Collect them into a portfolio.

*Average time: about two hours per case study.*

Use when you need examples of past work to share, or want to sharpen how you explain your work to non-practitioners.

### How to do it

1. **Answer four questions:**
   - What was the impact? Lead with this -- quantitative metrics if available, stakeholder or customer quotes if not. This is the only thing that compels a non-UX reader to keep reading, so put it first.
   - What were the project goals in one or two sentences? Frame the *why*, not the *what* -- "help more customers sign up and increase conversion rates," not "redesign the website."
   - What did you do, and who did you work with? A few high-level bullets are sufficient.
   - What is one interesting thing you learned? An "ah-ha" makes the case study memorable and sparks curiosity in the reader.
2. **Gather three to five visuals** -- final design images or notable working documents. Visuals draw people in and aid memory.
3. **Create a one-pager.** Combine write-up and images in a clean, formatted document that fits on one page.
4. **Memorize the story** so you can tell it fluently in conversation and send the document when someone asks for more.

### Inputs / Outputs

- **Inputs:** Completed project; any impact data or quotes; three to five images.
- **Outputs:** A one-page shareable document; a growing portfolio; a practiced verbal story.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Write for the businessperson, not the UX expert.** Business-side colleagues want to know: What problem did this solve? What impact did it produce? Was the investment worth it?
- **Give away the ending first.** Leading with outcome -- not process -- is what makes non-UX readers continue reading.

*For remote teams: especially well suited to remote practitioners because the artifact is self-contained and shares easily across distances.*

## Method 24: Peer-to-Peer Learning Community

### What it is and when to use it

An internal voluntary group where colleagues across roles gather to develop shared knowledge of UX. No one claims expert status -- the open, participatory tone is the point. Builds organizational UX literacy and surfaces potential collaborators. Use after initial awareness has been created via other methods.

*Average time: two to four hours of coordination effort per meeting for the organizer.*

### How to do it

1. **Invite broadly** -- explicitly including people in non-UX and non-product roles. Name a date and time for a kick-off meeting, in person when possible.
2. **Agree on goals and format at the first meeting.** Discuss what everyone hopes to learn. Then agree on a learning format. Options that work well individually or in combination:
   - A book club (read and discuss a UX book)
   - A discussion list (share examples and links asynchronously)
   - A brown bag / lunch-and-learn series (outside presenters or members present on UX topics)
   - A whitespace learning project (the group designs a solution to a shared problem)
3. **Close with commitments.** Agree on topic, format, frequency, and date of the next meeting before people leave.
4. **Assign or volunteer a leader.** Rotate leadership monthly to distribute the load. Send reminder emails between meetings.
5. **Iterate.** Check in with individual participants. Adjust format based on what is and is not working.

### Inputs / Outputs

- **Inputs:** A group of interested colleagues; a chosen format; recurring calendar time.
- **Outputs:** Colleagues with shared UX understanding; relationships that generate future project opportunities and advocacy.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Estimate prep time honestly.** Underestimating coordination overhead is the most common mistake. Budget realistically; consider a co-organizer.
- **Give it a time limit if uncertain.** A three-to-six-month pilot lowers the commitment threshold and creates urgency for participation.
- **Remote teams:** Asynchronous formats like a discussion list sustain momentum better than recurring video meetings.

## Method 25: Org Chart Evangelism

### What it is and when to use it

A permanent, ongoing track of relationship-building across all organizational levels -- individual contributors, mid-level managers, and senior leaders. The core mechanism is the one-on-one conversation (the lunch date is described as "your most powerful weapon"). Instead of winning an executive in one presentation, you build supporters at every level. Use after the "undercover UX" phase, when you are ready to build a recognized UX role or practice.

*Average time: ongoing.*

### How to do it

1. **Peers and approachable colleagues -- casual social conversation.** Ask someone to lunch, coffee, or a drink. Frame it as wanting their advice and input. Listen more than you talk. Find alignment between their priorities and UX capabilities. This is especially valuable with colleagues you have friction with -- understanding their pressures often resolves it.
2. **Mid-level managers -- capability sharing.** Ask if they would be interested in a quick walk-through of projects using new design techniques with the potential to help their team hit its goals. Schedule a half-hour, keep it casual, and leave time for questions.
3. **Senior leaders -- indirect and project-status routes.** Use existing project update meetings to share outcomes with senior stakeholders, or build advocates at lower levels who can carry your message upward.
4. **In all cases:** connect what you share to the results, KPIs, and priorities the other person already cares about.

### Inputs / Outputs

- **Inputs:** Mini case studies (Method 23) to share; genuine curiosity about others' work; patience.
- **Outputs:** A distributed base of supporters across the org; inbound project opportunities; trust and goodwill built over time.

### Example from the book

John Griffin, Director of Customer Experience Design for Blue Cross NC: "Doing great work, at whatever level you have permission to play at, is the non-negotiable thing that gets you in the door to have bigger conversations."

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Give it time.** A UX practice is built through relationships, trust, and goodwill over time -- not by delivering great projects alone. Expect progress to be measured in months.
- **One conversation is never enough.** A positive lunch is the beginning of a relationship, not its conclusion.

*For remote teams: plan in-person conversations when occasional visits allow. A sincere, personal email invitation for a video call also works -- remote meetings are now commonplace.*

## "If You Only Do One Thing"

Focus on **Org Chart Evangelism**. If the full org chart is overwhelming, identify a few key potential supporters and invite them to lunch. That one step lays the foundation for future discussions, future work together, and -- critically -- the feeling that even as a UX team of one, you are not alone.

---

## Best Practices

- Connect every UX message to the goals and KPIs of the audience, never to UX craft for its own sake.
- Keep all evangelist artifacts short and self-explanatory: one-page flyers, one-page case studies, thirty-minute walkthroughs.
- Treat every conversation as a listening exercise first. Understand what others are trying to accomplish, then offer alignment.
- Approach skeptics and colleagues you have friction with early -- resolving friction early compounds goodwill.
- Rotate Captive UX messages every few months to maintain novelty.

---

## Real-World Application

**If a user asks how to get buy-in for UX in their organization:** walk them through the four-method arc in sequence -- start with Captive UX or Mini Case Studies to create initial awareness with zero organizational friction, use a Peer-to-Peer Learning Community to formalize interest into an ongoing forum, and run Org Chart Evangelism in parallel as a long-term relationship track.

**If a user asks how to document or share their UX work:** apply the Mini Case Study structure -- lead with impact, frame goals as business outcomes, select visuals, keep it to one page, memorize it for verbal delivery.

**If a user asks how to reach a specific stakeholder:** map them to the Org Chart Evangelism approach -- casual social for peers, capability-sharing walkthrough for mid-level managers, project-status or proxy channels for senior leaders.
