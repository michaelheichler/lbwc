# Research Methods

Chapter 5 of "The User Experience Team of One" by Leah Buley and Joe Natoli gives you six investigative methods for learning what users need, want, and experience -- covering everything from scoping your knowledge gaps to auditing product content. These methods are designed to be executed by a single practitioner, often under time and access constraints, without sacrificing rigor of insight. Their shared purpose is to help you see the product with fresh eyes rather than the apologetic familiarity of someone too close to it.

---

## Method 7: Learning Plan

### What it is and when to use it

A learning plan maps out what you know about your users, separates confident knowledge from working assumptions, and identifies how to fill the gaps. Use it when you are new to a team or project, when you are bringing a user-centered mindset to an organization for the first time, or at the start of any project where your knowledge of user needs is unclear.

**Time required:** 1--2 hours to assemble; ongoing discussion afterward.

### How to do it (step by step)

1. **Dump your assumptions.** Set aside 30--60 minutes to list everything you think you know about users without editing. Cover: user segments and types, motivations, frustrations, important features, purchase/reuse factors, competitor product use, time spent with your product per week/month/year, what competes for their attention, and which product areas they use most vs. least.
2. **Separate certainties from assumptions.** For each item, rate your confidence honestly. Look for questions that can be grouped together.
3. **Brainstorm research methods.** For each knowledge gap, identify how you might fill it -- face-to-face interviews, surveys, analytics, call-center transcripts, search logs. Not all questions need direct user access; be creative about indirect data sources.
4. **Plan outputs.** For each research area, decide what form the evidence will take (charts, distributed personas, infographics, etc.) before you start.
5. **Make it glanceable.** Format the plan as a table -- one page -- that can go on a wall or into a presentation deck.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Your current assumptions, any existing research, team knowledge
- **Outputs:** A structured table of assumptions + confidence levels + research methods + planned deliverables

### Example from the book

A team starting a new product project holds a 60-minute whiteboard session with a few colleagues to brainstorm and capture their working assumptions about users. The result is a one-page table they can stick on the wall and revisit quarterly.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Share it for feedback.** Getting a boss or team to trim your ambitions is productive -- it sharpens focus. A plan no one has seen cannot get support.
- **Revisit regularly.** Companies routinely make design decisions based on research that is years old. Users, technology, and behavior change. Revalidate assumptions at least annually, ideally quarterly.
- **Remote adaptation:** Prioritize methods executable at a distance -- Zoom interviews, Slack chats, remote usability tests.

## Method 8: Guerilla User Research

### What it is and when to use it

The one non-negotiable of user research: you must actually talk to users. Even two or three is better than none. Guerilla user research takes any form -- DMs, Zoom calls, coffee shop intercepts, Slack conversations -- to get firsthand data quickly. Use it when the team is making decisions without primary user knowledge, when you are stuck in design arguments, or when people are speculating about fictitious users instead of consulting real ones.

**Time required:** ~2 days total (3--4 hours prep, 3--4 hours in field, 4--6 hours analyzing data).

### How to do it (step by step)

1. **Define your target users.** Characterize them by age, behaviors, interests, location, how they spend time. Then identify where they physically or digitally congregate.
2. **List research questions using a mind map.** Write your core topic in the center; branch out with first-level questions, then sub-questions, until exhausted.
3. **Go into the field.** Meet users in their own environment when possible. Ask them to show you what is relevant. Take photos and videos (with consent). Cover all your listed questions during the conversation.
4. **Mine the data.** After all sessions, review notes across interviews looking for answers to your original questions and recurring patterns.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** User characterization, list of research questions, a consent form
- **Outputs:** Field notes, photos/videos, patterns and insights tied back to research questions

### Example from the book

If your target customers are creative freelancers who work in coffee shops, spend an afternoon there and politely ask laptop workers if they would participate in a short study. Ask about what they *do* and how they *complete tasks*, not about which software they use -- because process, policy, and environmental factors are often the real source of UX problems.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Always get informed consent in writing** before any session.
- **Ask open-ended questions** starting with "why" or "how." Avoid yes/no questions. The single most powerful follow-up: "Why?"
- **Do not ask about the screen or product directly.** Ask what they do and how they accomplish a task. Software may be a symptom; the real problem may be policy, process, or stress.
- **Ask about past events chronologically** ("Tell me about the last time you..."). A journey-line exercise -- drawing the highs and lows on paper -- helps users surface specifics they would otherwise gloss over.
- **Allow silence.** Do not fill pauses or suggest answers. Let them think.
- **If time is blocked,** ask for less: 15-minute sessions with several users in a day. Record and transcribe. Look for patterns. Something is always better than nothing.
- **Use your organization.** Sales and support teams, LinkedIn, conference attendee lists, marketing newsletters -- these are all access channels for participants.
- **Offer compensation.** Even a small gift card increases willingness to participate.
- **Share with visceral evidence.** Include choice photos and audio clips when presenting findings to make the user voice tangible.

## Method 9: User Archetypes

### What it is and when to use it

User archetypes are a lightweight alternative to full personas (personas being a technique invented by Alan Cooper). Where personas require extensive firsthand research, archetypes can be built from secondhand research or well-informed team hunches -- making them a "persona hack" that any cross-functional group can create in a few hours. Use them when your picture of target users is unclear, when the team talks about users in vague generic terms, or when you need to build shared empathy quickly.

**Time required:** 3--6 hours (2--4 hours prep, 1--2 hours workshop).

### How to do it (step by step)

1. **Schedule a working session** with 4--6 people from a cross-functional group. Include people with direct customer access -- sales, support.
2. **Set expectations.** Explain that this is an unscientific but effective technique for building empathetic, user-oriented thinking. Frame UX as a mindset the group is adopting together.
3. **Form small teams** of about three. Each team discusses what they know or suspect about users and picks one user type to develop.
4. **Fill in the archetype.** Give each team a poster-sized template. They create a fictitious but realistic person: name, location, job, frustrations, a day in their life, what they do before/during/after using the product.
5. **Bring it to life.** Add photos from free stock sites (pexels.com, unsplash.com, stocksnap.io) and representative quotes.
6. **Share and discuss.** Each team presents their archetype. Group discusses: What questions does it raise? Which archetypes are most effective for empathy? How will these be used going forward?

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Any pre-existing customer research, market segments, support insights, or team knowledge
- **Outputs:** Poster-sized user archetype profiles representing distinct user types; a shared team vocabulary for discussing users

### Example from the book

Individual interview notes captured on sticky notes can be compiled into a single archetype profile -- a lower-fidelity version suitable when you won't be sharing the result widely, or when conducting the exercise remotely.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Base them on whatever research you have.** Even informal team knowledge beats pure invention.
- **Treat them as a hypothesis,** not ground truth. Validate against field data when the opportunity arises.
- **Do not confuse with real personas.** If the opportunity for proper persona development (grounded in observed research) arises, take it. Archetypes are a bridge, not a destination.
- **Remote adaptation:** The workshop is harder but still worth doing over Zoom. If group collaboration is impossible, creating archetypes solo still yields guidance for your own design thinking.

## Method 10: Heuristic Markup

### What it is and when to use it

A heuristic markup is a structured walkthrough of your product from a user's starting point to completion, recording thoughts, reactions, and emotions at each step. It is inspired by formal heuristic evaluations but emphasizes gut reactions and experiential story over standards compliance. Use it to assess overall product quality, spot improvement opportunities, or check work-in-progress designs.

**Time required:** 4--6 hours to conduct and document.

### How to do it (step by step)

1. **Block sufficient time.** Plan more than you think you need so you can move slowly and capture thoughts.
2. **Start where users start.** A Google search? The app store? Unboxing a device? Approach from the same angle a first-timer would.
3. **Document as you go.** Paste screenshots into a Google Doc (or use a screen recorder with audio like Loom or Camtasia) and add notes about what you notice and what a user's mindset likely is at each step.
4. **Pay attention to subtle reactions.** Slow down deliberately. Your inner voice has reactions -- catch them before they pass. Record both thoughts and emotions (the seven universal emotions: anger, joy, sadness, disgust, fear, contempt, surprise).
5. **Continue through to completion.** Cover the full experience, not just the happy path.
6. **Summarize and share.** End with a short list of the most critical findings. Soften language before sharing -- you are critiquing someone's hard work. Capture the feeling; leave the expletives out.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Access to the live product; optionally, user archetypes to adopt as a persona lens
- **Outputs:** Annotated screenshot document or screen recording; emotional journey graph; prioritized list of improvement areas

### Example from the book

Walk through a product adopting a user archetype's backstory. At each screen, note what you think and feel. Draw a journey line marking the emotional highs and lows of the experience to pinpoint the most critical moments to improve or preserve.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Adopt a persona.** Using a user archetype as a lens during the walkthrough keeps your observations grounded in realistic user goals rather than your own expert knowledge.
- **Make an emotional graph.** Map the seven universal emotions across the experience to visualize where friction is worst.
- **Back up observations with recognized standards** when stakeholders push back on "gut feeling" critiques: Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics, Tog's principles of interaction design, Apple HIG, or the Atlassian Design System are credible references.
- **Task-based variant:** Instead of start-to-finish, identify a handful of core use cases and audit each individually.
- **Invite colleagues.** Having others walk through the product firsthand builds empathy far more effectively than hearing your summary.

## Method 11: Comparative Assessment

### What it is and when to use it

A comparative assessment evaluates *indirect* competitors -- products users encounter in everyday life that have nothing to do with your domain (Amazon, Spotify, YouTube) -- because these shape user expectations more broadly than direct competitors do. It differs from a competitive assessment (which evaluates direct competitors feature-for-feature). Use it when establishing vision for a new UX, when the team is fixated on feature parity, or when you hear vague aspirations like "make it the Apple of [category]" without specifics.

**Time required:** 4--8 hours (4--6 hours reviewing products, optional 1--2 hours writing a guidelines document).

### How to do it (step by step)

1. **Pick 3--5 products** for your assessment -- a manageable number covering a range of experience quality and relevant interaction patterns.
2. **Define your evaluation framework.** Use a spreadsheet with rows for each product and columns for key evaluation areas: content, design, features/functionality, continuity/flow, performance/responsiveness, intuitiveness, and strengths/weaknesses/opportunities.
3. **Walk through each product with fresh eyes.** Take screenshots and note your assessment as if encountering it for the first time.
4. **Fill in the framework.** Once all products are assessed, compare across them in the spreadsheet.
5. **Summarize findings as guidelines.** Document key insights as design principles or standards your product should meet or exceed. This becomes a cheat sheet for the team and can feed directly into a Design Brief.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** List of comparator products, evaluation framework, your own use of each product
- **Outputs:** Completed assessment spreadsheet; optional guidelines document with annotated screenshots

### Example from the book

If a team keeps saying "it should be easy like Amazon," a comparative assessment makes that concrete: you walk through Amazon, note specifically what makes navigation intuitive, how content is prioritized, how the experience flows across devices -- then translate those observations into actionable design standards for your own product.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Do not create feature-comparison lists.** That path leads to feature bloat. Focus on the quality, harmony, and consistency of the overall experience -- flow, readability, intuitiveness, cross-device continuity -- not on which features exist.
- **Design the report format first.** Deciding what you want to communicate at the end helps you know what questions to ask during the assessment.
- **Make it a group exercise.** Ask participants to bring their own suggested comparators and review them together. Shared discovery is more valuable than a solo deliverable.
- **Remote adaptation:** This is one of the methods that works well as a solo remote activity. Share findings via Zoom with screensharing.

## Method 12: Content Patterns

### What it is and when to use it

A content patterns analysis is a focused alternative to a full content inventory (which is comprehensive but exhausting). Instead of cataloging everything, you identify recurring structural patterns -- the repeating templates of content and functionality that make up the product -- and assess their quality, consistency, and alignment with user expectations. Use it at the start of a project to develop intimate familiarity with what a product actually contains and how it is organized.

**Time required:** 4--8 hours (2--4 hours reviewing patterns, 1--2 hours mapping/spreadsheet, 1--2 hours summarizing findings).

### How to do it (step by step)

1. **Pick key sections** of the product that represent a meaningful cross-section of content types.
2. **Find the patterns.** For each section, list every element that makes up a unit of content. For example, an article has: title, byline, date, author, paragraphs, images, tags, categories. Repeat for each content type you find.
3. **Note mismatches.** Look for discrepancies in labeling, language, hierarchy, and priority -- differences between what users expect and what they get, what things are called vs. how users would name them.
4. **Document in a spreadsheet or map.** A diagram or content map often makes structural relationships more visible than a list alone.
5. **Summarize key findings.** Identify top observations, discrepancies, and specific recommendations. What should be changed and why?
6. **Share and discuss.** Present findings to the team and agree on actions.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Access to the live product or staging environment
- **Outputs:** Content pattern spreadsheet or map; written summary of key findings and recommendations

### Example from the book

A UX practitioner working on a help-center redesign walks through all help content types -- articles, FAQs, video tutorials, release notes -- and discovers that "articles" and "guides" are used interchangeably to mean different things in different sections. This mismatch in labeling creates navigation confusion that would have been invisible without the pattern analysis.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Summarize, do not present the spreadsheet.** A raw content inventory puts audiences to sleep. Lead with the insight, not the data. Give away the ending first: what you found, what it means, and what you recommend.
- **Show real examples.** Use in-product screenshots and, where applicable, examples from competing products that already solve the problem well.
- **Remote adaptation:** Share findings via Zoom with screensharing. Focus on outcomes and examples, not exhaustive enumeration.

## Best Practices

- Research is not a phase -- it is an ongoing discipline. Revisit your Learning Plan at least quarterly; user behavior and technology shift faster than product teams expect.
- Even one user conversation is better than none. Never let perfect access block you from imperfect but real data.
- Always get informed consent in writing before any user session, without exception.
- When sharing research findings, lead with the insight and the recommendation; back up with evidence. Audiences tune out spreadsheets and raw transcripts.
- Triangulate across methods: a Heuristic Markup reveals what is broken in the UI; Guerilla Research reveals why users struggle in ways the UI cannot fully explain.
- Treat user archetypes and comparative assessments as hypotheses and design targets, not as finished truth. Validate them when you can.
- The core discipline across all six methods: look past the familiar in order to see the product with fresh eyes.

## Real-World Application

**If a user asks you to audit their product or website:**
Use the Heuristic Markup steps -- start where their users start, walk the full flow, document reactions and emotions at each step, and return a prioritized list of issues with a journey-line graph of highs and lows. Back sharp observations with Nielsen's heuristics or platform design guidelines to pre-empt "that's just your opinion" pushback.

**If a user needs to understand their users better but has no research budget or time:**
Run a User Archetypes workshop outline -- prompt them with the template questions (name, job, frustrations, day in life, before/during/after product use) and help them populate 2--3 archetypes from whatever team knowledge exists. Flag these as hypotheses to validate.

**If a user is starting a new project and does not know where to begin:**
Build a Learning Plan together -- list their current assumptions across the key question areas, rate confidence, identify gaps, and map a lightweight research approach for each gap using whatever access they have (analytics, support tickets, a single Zoom call with one customer).

**If a user wants to benchmark their UX against competitors:**
Run a Comparative Assessment -- help them pick 3--5 indirect comparators that set user expectations in their space, define an evaluation framework across content/design/flow/intuitiveness, and summarize findings as a one-page guidelines document they can use to steer design decisions.

## "If You Only Do One Thing..."

Do user research. It is the fastest path to seeing the world through your users' eyes -- revealing both areas for improvement and a concrete sense of who your users are and what matters to them most.
