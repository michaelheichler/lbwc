# Planning and Discovery Methods

Six methods for focusing and aligning a UX effort before design work begins. This chapter gives you tools to answer the questions that -- if left unanswered -- reliably cause trouble: what are we fixing, why does it matter, who are the users, what does success look like, do we all agree. Each method is lightweight enough for a solo practitioner and structured enough to build real alignment with non-UX colleagues.

---

## Method 1: UX Questionnaire

**What it is:** A standard list of questions you ask yourself about a product at the start of any engagement. Use before any new project, or whenever you feel uncertain about the fundamentals. Surfaces red flags -- misaligned strategy, undefined users, unclear success metrics -- before those problems contaminate design.

**How to do it:**
1. Block one hour. Work through each category in writing -- rough notes count.
2. **Project Goals:** Summarize as 3-5 statements. Dozens of goals signals scope is too large; ask stakeholders to rank priorities.
3. **Users:** Describe target users in detail: who they are, why they use the product, primary pain points. "Our users are everybody" is a red flag.
4. **Strategy:** Define the value proposition and what level of feature parity is required to compete.
5. **Tasks, technology, scenarios:** List primary tasks the design must support, current tools users rely on, platform/device/environment constraints, locked-in technical decisions.
6. **Success measures:** How the product makes money, which user behaviors drive profit, active KPIs.
7. **Key dates:** Firm deadlines and what is driving them.
8. **Risks:** Obstacles, adoption blockers, legal/compliance constraints.
9. For any unanswered question, ask two or three colleagues -- different people give different perspectives.

**Inputs:** Any existing project documentation, willingness to ask a few colleagues.
**Output:** A completed scratch-pad covering all categories. Feeds directly into the Project Brief (Method 5).

**Pitfalls:** Do not skip because the project "seems obvious" -- that is exactly when unstated assumptions cause damage. Remote practitioners: gaps are harder to notice at a distance, making this even more important.

## Method 2: UX Project Plan

**What it is:** Your personal plan for how you will conduct UX activities -- distinct from the PM's overall project plan. Worth creating whenever your involvement spans more than a few weeks. Forces honesty about what you will actually do, and sets clear expectations for non-UX colleagues. Even if no one asked for it, having one ready builds credibility.

**How to do it:**
1. **Confirm goals:** Summarize in a few short statements focused on the business or behavioral change you are creating.
2. **Brainstorm methods by working backward:** If the goal is more inbound leads → better landing pages → user research on current perceptions → research plan. Follow the chain.
3. **Estimate duration** for each activity, then rate each 1 (simple) to 3 (complex/unpredictable). Pad time for items rated 3.
4. **Place milestones:** Talk to colleagues about firm deadlines. A four-week prototype deadline might mean guerrilla user research instead of in-field contextual research.
5. **Write a simple document:** Activities, durations, inputs, outputs, who is involved. A whiteboard works fine.
6. **Plan triage buffer zones:** After research (when findings are being socialized) and when designs move to higher fidelity (when stakeholder reactions often trigger revisions).

**Inputs:** Confirmed goals, key milestones.
**Output:** A document showing UX activities, durations, responsibilities, and milestone touchpoints.

**Pitfalls:** Your first draft will be unrealistically paced -- plan a second pass to scope it down. Use a standard UX process checklist to avoid accidentally skipping a phase.

## Method 3: Stakeholder Interviews

**What it is:** One-on-one informal conversations with colleagues across functions to learn their priorities, assumptions about users, product concerns, and appetite for UX improvement. Use when joining a new team, starting a large project, or building a case for UX involvement.

**How to do it:**
1. **Define learning objectives:** Two or three things you most need to understand.
2. **Build your interviewee list by role:** Who holds budget, who engineers it, markets it, sells it, supports it, has veto power over design? Aim for cross-functional coverage.
3. **Write an interview guide.** Core questions: What is your role and how does it relate to the product? What are your goals and how do you measure success? Who are your customers and what do they need? Who are your competitors? (If a specific project is underway) What are your goals and what risks do you see?
4. **Schedule sessions.** Offer 30 minutes -- harder to refuse. Meeting over coffee keeps the tone natural.
5. **Take good notes.** Type if fast; bring a note-taker if not. Avoid recording stakeholders -- it makes people cautious. Reserve the last few minutes to have them summarize their key points.
6. **Synthesize:** Extract themes on expectations of UX, user assumptions, product problems, past failures, open questions, who wants involvement.

**Inputs:** Stakeholder list, short interview guide, two or three learning objectives.
**Output:** A synthesized theme set covering priorities, user assumptions, political constraints, and UX support levels.

**Example:** If nearly all stakeholders cite cutting support costs as their chief concern, frame findings accordingly: "Top 3 ways UX improvement reduces support costs" -- give the ending first, then the evidence.

**Pitfalls:** Accept what people say, but keep asking "why?" to surface what is really underneath. Share a thank-you and theme summary after -- this builds goodwill. Remote: use video (Zoom, Google Meet, Teams); up to 80% of communication is nonverbal.

> **"If You Only Do One Thing":** The book names stakeholder interviews as the single most valuable method in this chapter. They expose organizational priorities, political considerations, team dynamics, and the assumptions you will be working within as you try to improve the product.

## Method 4: Opportunity Workshop

**What it is:** A two-hour facilitated session with a cross-functional team to surface UX problems, missed opportunities, product strengths, and then prioritize them. Use when discussions about needing better UX are happening but there is no clear momentum or agreed starting point.

**How to do it:**
1. Invite a cross-functional group; block at least two hours.
2. **Pain storm:** Everyone writes sticky notes (one item per note) for: problems in the current product, missed opportunities, things that must be done right. Share and post each note.
3. **Discuss strengths:** Repeat the sticky-note exercise for strengths. Ending on positives balances the critical tone.
4. **Find themes:** Guide the group to cluster related notes and label each cluster (different color for labels). Issues become broad themes rather than isolated complaints.
5. **Prioritize:** Give everyone votes (dots) to place on the clusters they think are most urgent.
6. **Discuss next steps** based on visible priorities.

**Inputs:** Cross-functional group, sticky notes, wall or whiteboard.
**Output:** Prioritized problem themes and strengths, with initial next-step ideas for decision-maker conversations.

**Pitfalls:** This is not a substitute for user research -- treat all identified issues as hypotheses. Remote option: Zoom session, or a two-round survey (strengths/weaknesses first, prioritization second) via SurveyMonkey or Google Forms.

## Method 5: Project Brief

**What it is:** A one-page document stating expected outcomes -- what you are doing, why, and the constraints, in that order. Use when starting a new project or when joining an in-progress project that lacks shared clarity. Brevity is the point: people read it, and it forces alignment or productive disagreement before design begins.

**How to do it:**
1. Answer the core questions (pull from your UX Questionnaire if done): business needs and measurable impact; user needs and known unknowns; goals (distinguish the *what* from the *how* -- "reduce support calls" is the what, "redesign the self-service UI" is the how); key expectations and any role discrepancies.
2. Write a short document -- one page maximum -- structured as: what you are building, who it is for, what the experience should ultimately deliver.
3. Circulate for input. If email produces no response, turn it into a meeting or visit people.
4. Revisit at major milestones to track progress against the agreed outcomes.

**Inputs:** UX Questionnaire answers, stakeholder interview themes.
**Output:** A one-page shared document the team has agreed to as the project mandate.

**Example:** The book recommends a "redlining workshop" to make review ceremonial: print the brief at 2' × 3' poster size, give everyone red pens, and facilitate live editing. Remote equivalent: Miro or FigJam with live markup.

**Pitfalls:** Apply the rule of three -- no more than three business needs, three user needs, three features to address. A laundry list defeats the purpose. Confusing the what with the how in the goals section is the most common brief-writing error.

## Method 6: Strategy Workshop

**What it is:** A two-to-four-hour facilitated session to establish a shared product vision and prioritize focus areas. Use when a product feels like a loose collection of features, when the team cannot agree on direction, or when you do not know what differentiates the product from competitors. Best run before development begins, when openness to strategic thinking is highest.

**How to do it:**

First, decide workshop goals: articulate what makes the product unique; create an inspiring future vision; prioritize features; determine now/next/later focus.

Then select activities to match:

**Triads (for uniqueness):** Brainstorm keywords the product should embody. Select three words that together describe the core experience. For each triad, generate nouns (objects in the system), verbs (actions to support), adjectives (how it should feel). Create two or three different triads and discuss which feels most right -- allow at least 30 minutes per discussion.

**Elevator Pitch (for uniqueness):** Use a fill-in-the-blank template (adapted from Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* via Adaptive Path) to draft a succinct statement of the product's differentiation and value. Teams fill in the template independently, then compare.

**Artifact from the Future (for vision):** Ask team members to imagine the product is live and complete. Have them write a press release, blog post, or customer testimonial for it. Share artifacts and discuss what still needs to be built and what personality the product must have.

**Storyboards (for vision):** Break into groups; each sketches a user scenario in stick figures. Different groups naturally explore different aspects of the experience, giving you breadth.

**Mood Boards (for vision):** Large collages of colors, photos, and catchphrases representing how the finished product should feel. Serves as a reference touchstone throughout development.

**2x2 or Kano Model (for prioritization):** Plot features on cost-versus-impact axes (2x2), or use the Kano model axes (satisfaction vs. achievement) to identify basic attributes (must-haves), performance attributes (competitive requirements), and delight attributes (unexpected but loved). In this workshop context, the Kano model facilitates team discussion rather than collecting direct user feedback.

**Inputs:** Cross-functional team, workshop goals, selected activities.
**Output:** Shared vision, product identity words, prioritized feature focus, documented alignment and divergence points.

**Pitfalls:** Match activities to goals -- triads and elevator pitch for uniqueness questions; artifacts, storyboards, mood boards for vision; 2x2 or Kano for prioritization. Run in person when possible; the book recommends traveling for this. Users prefer fewer features executed extremely well -- prioritization in the workshop protects against feature bloat.

## Best Practices

- Sequence the methods: Questionnaire and Stakeholder Interviews first, then Project Brief and UX Project Plan, then Opportunity or Strategy Workshop for alignment.
- Any of the seven questionnaire questions left unanswered at project start is a leading indicator of trouble.
- Distinguish symptoms from causes. The UI is where organizational problems become visible; the root is usually strategy gaps, misaligned teams, or political dynamics.
- Keep deliverables short. Polishing planning artifacts is avoidance.
- Share findings generously. Summaries to interviewees, circulated briefs, visible plans -- this builds goodwill you will need when asking for time, access, and resources.
- An Opportunity Workshop is not a substitute for user research. Team perspectives are hypotheses; users are evidence.

## Real-World Application

When a user brings Claude a product problem ("our conversion rate is low," "users are not adopting this feature," "we need to redesign onboarding"), apply this chapter's logic before jumping to solutions:

1. **Method 1 framing:** Ask about goals, users, success metrics, risks. Surface unanswered questions explicitly.
2. **Method 3 framing:** Who are the stakeholders whose priorities need to be understood? Help the user draft an interview guide and suggest who to talk to.
3. **Method 5 structure:** Help the user write a one-paragraph brief -- what, for whom, to what measurable end -- to test whether there is real alignment on goals.
4. **Method 6 activities:** If the team is misaligned on product direction, recommend triads, elevator pitch, or artifact from the future as workshop exercises.
5. **Method 2 plan:** For any multi-week engagement, help build a plan with activities, estimates, milestones, and triage buffers.

The throughline: planning and discovery are not overhead -- they are the work that makes all subsequent work coherent.
