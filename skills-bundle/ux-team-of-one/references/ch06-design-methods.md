# Design Methods

Chapter 6 of *The User Experience Team of One* covers the core design-phase methods that move you from research insights to a concrete, shareable product design. It centers on iterative, inclusive work -- bringing non-UX colleagues into the process so they become co-owners rather than critics. The methods span from high-level design intent (Design Brief) through flow thinking (Task Flows) and rapid visual exploration (Sketching) to detailed structural specification (Wireframes).

---

## Method 13: Design Brief

### What it is and when to use it

A design brief documents your working assumptions about the optimal design direction for a product. It articulates the problems UX/UI improvement will solve (or the opportunities it will create) and connects the dots on how that becomes reality. It is the place where UX and design principles live -- short statements that answer: *What should the experience of using the product look and feel like to a user, and how does the design create it?*

Use it when:
- You want to align the team's expectations about design before work begins.
- You need to set a standard for how the product should feel and what distinguishes it from competitors.
- You need a foundation for a design system.
- The vision or personality for the product is unclear.

**Average time:** 3--5 hours total (2--3 hours to draft; 1--2 hours to share and incorporate feedback).

### Step-by-step how-to

1. **Focus** -- State what will be created or improved and why. What problem does this design work solve?
2. **Audience** -- Define who you are designing for (primary personas) and, just as importantly, who you are *not* designing for. Include enough description to surface differing assumptions among teammates.
3. **Features and functionality** -- List core screens, states, or scenarios that will be most useful and valuable to users.
4. **Feeling** -- Describe the emotion users should feel when using the product. Listen for adjectives that stakeholders and colleagues naturally use in conversation -- write them down and put them here.
5. **Uniqueness** -- Identify differentiators and special characteristics that set the product apart. Draw from any research already completed.
6. **UX and design principles** -- Draft no more than five short, human-voice statements about what the finished product should be like. Each should be specific enough that it *could not describe any competing product*. Avoid generic statements like "easy to use" -- those are table stakes.
7. **Restrictions and expectations** -- Capture constraints: technology/OS platforms, device form factors, accessibility requirements, and things that absolutely cannot be compromised.
8. **Share and discuss** -- Present the brief (in draft form) to the whole team. Frame it as a conversation starter, not a decree. Actively solicit feedback and use it to reach alignment before design begins.
9. **Refer back continually** -- As you move through sketching, wireframes, and prototyping, ask at each step: "Does what I'm designing feel like this principle?" If not, something needs to change.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Research findings, stakeholder conversations, product strategy documents, competitive context.
- **Outputs:** A brief document covering focus, audience, features, feeling, uniqueness, design principles (max five), and constraints. Optionally enriched with imagery that gives emotional resonance to the principles.

### Real example (from the book)

The UK government's design principles are cited as an exemplary model. The principle "This is for everyone" gives teams and stakeholders clear, pointed guidance on which side to err on when tough choices arise -- and clearly defines what distinguishes its products and what matters most to its user base. A principle like that passes the test: it could not describe just any competing product.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **No generic principles.** Test every principle by asking: could this describe any competing product? If yes, sharpen it.
- **Go beyond "easy to use."** Simple and friendly are table stakes in modern UX -- principles must do more.
- **Add images.** Imagery gives visual and emotional resonance and helps the team connect to the brief and remember it.
- **Make it a group activity.** Generate a big list of candidate principles, then workshop the list with the team to narrow and refine it. Non-UX colleagues become invested co-authors rather than passive recipients.
- **For remote teams:** Share the draft early, frame it as a conversation, and run a real-time working session via Zoom/Teams plus a collaborative whiteboard tool.

## Method 14: Task Flows

### What it is and when to use it

A task flow maps how an experience unfolds over time -- the sequences users follow, the decision points they hit, and the alternate paths they may take. It gets you thinking about how people *really* use a product: they pop in through the back door, jump erratically, and break your logic. The places where UX breaks down most often are the transition moments between steps or screens -- and a task flow is specifically designed to surface and solve those.

Use it when you need a complete picture of how the broader user experience hangs together, for yourself and for your team.

**Average time:** 1--2 hours for a specific task flow. Service blueprints (front- and back-end combined) can take weeks.

### Step-by-step how-to

1. **Figure out your starting point.** Choose one of three approaches:
   - *Top-down:* Start with what a user encounters first and work forward.
   - *Bottom-up:* Start with the "moments of truth" -- key destinations or transition points (e.g., purchase confirmation on an e-commerce site) -- and map what comes before and after.
   - *Scenario-based:* Blend both by tracing how a user starts at the top and works their way to the key moments.
2. **Draw or depict the initial screen or step** as a small, thumbnail-sized representation -- just enough detail to convey the essence of the screen and the interactions available.
3. **Consider what comes next (or prior).** Work outward from your starting point, repeating until you reach a reasonable start-and-end boundary.
4. **Think about alternate entries and exits.** For each step: Is there another way to get here? Where else might a user go from here? What happens if they abandon? If they return later or on a different device, what should they see?
5. **Think about decision points and branches.** Where does a user choose between options? Where does the user's role determine what they can see or do (e.g., employee vs. manager)? Map how different paths diverge and converge.
6. **Add annotations.** Go back through and note how transitions should work, and capture anything that is not self-evident from the visual flow alone.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Research findings, design brief, knowledge of core user scenarios.
- **Outputs:** A whiteboard diagram (or lightweight digital equivalent) of boxes and arrows showing screens/steps, transitions, decision points, alternate paths, and annotations. Can also be represented as low-fidelity sketched UI screens connected by arrows.

### Real example (from the book)

An e-commerce site's purchase and confirmation screens are given as a "moment of truth" example. Starting from that point bottom-up, you map what happens before (product page, cart, checkout) and after (order confirmation, email receipt) -- then add the alternate paths: what if the user is not logged in? What if they return to a half-filled cart on a different device?

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Draw it on a whiteboard.** Analog methods keep everyone focused on the flow, not the aesthetics. The rough format prevents being precious about the work and prevents premature assumptions about what something will look like. This is called out as "mission-critical" in the book.
- **Keep it lightweight.** Use sticky notes on large paper for maximum flexibility -- each note is one screen; rearrange freely.
- **Don't boil the ocean.** Create discrete flows for core scenarios rather than one massive diagram for the entire system. Connect individual flows afterward.
- **Use as a prototype starting point.** A task flow directly informs which screens to include in a clickable prototype and when interactivity should occur.
- **Multichannel experiences:** If the flow crosses channels or has non-digital components, consider an experience blueprint (from service design) instead.
- **For remote teams:** Do initial thinking solo, then run a group working session via Zoom + Miro to walk through and refine. Engineers are especially good at identifying edge cases and alternate paths.

## Method 15: Sketching

### What it is and when to use it

Sketching is when you sit down with pen and paper (or a digital equivalent) and draw out your ideas for what the user interface could look like. In UX, it has added significance: it is the point in the process where you explore different potential forms the design could take before committing to higher-fidelity work.

Use it when:
- You are starting to explore design ideas.
- You want to engage the team in a tangible discussion of possible directions.
- You are unsure which direction the design should go in.

**Average time:** As little as 30 focused minutes; 1--3 hours for group sessions including review and next-step decisions.

### Step-by-step how-to

1. **Choose your tools.** Whatever lets you think and draw quickly: pencil and paper, a special sketchbook, an iPad and stylus, or drawing over screenshots in Miro/FigJam/Figma. Clarity matters; beauty does not.
2. **Use screen templates.** Sketch on templates with accurate sizing and aspect ratios for the target device (smartphone, tablet, browser). This keeps sketches grounded in realistic constraints early.
3. **Set a time constraint.** Give yourself 30 minutes. The time limit forces quantity over quality -- the goal is to generate ideas, not polish them.
4. **Sketch first ideas for key moments.** Ask: what is the most interesting or valuable moment in this product for the user? Start there and sketch potential screen layouts.
5. **Sketch alternatives.** After one or two ideas, force yourself to sketch at least a few additional approaches. If the first was text-heavy, try a primarily visual version. The book recommends exploring six different alternatives per screen to push past obvious first ideas.
6. **Review, critique, and revise.** Assemble team members or colleagues. Each person presents their ideas and solicits feedback. Start by explaining what informed your thinking, then walk through the sketches. Encourage honest, off-the-cuff feedback. Be 100% willing to be wrong.
7. **Identify promising ideas.** By the end, determine which ideas are worth developing into higher-fidelity designs (more detailed sketches, wireframes, or prototypes). Mix and match the best elements from multiple sketches.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Design brief, task flows, any research findings, screen templates.
- **Outputs:** A collection of rough sketch explorations for key screens or moments, annotated with notes (preferably in a contrasting color). After review: a shortlist of promising directions to carry forward.

### Real example (from the book)

In a redesign scenario, projecting current screens on a whiteboard and drawing over them with whiteboard markers is recommended. This makes it immediately tangible for people who are used to reviewing polished designs -- they can see exactly what elements are not working and how you plan to improve them.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Clarity before beauty.** Sketches do not need to look good; they need to be understandable. Add annotations in a different color (e.g., red) with arrows to clarify what squiggles mean.
- **Stick to basic shapes.** Most UI elements can be drawn with rectangles, lines, and simple icons. Do not get distracted by rendering.
- **Sketch in words if stuck.** Describing an idea in words before drawing it can unlock thinking for those who are naturally word-oriented.
- **Know your altitude.** Decide the scope before you start: are you doing a 30,000-foot system-wide exploration or a 5-foot dive into a single screen state? Mixing altitudes in a single session causes confusion.
- **Get people on their feet.** Group sketching sessions are participatory and physical -- enforce a "no butts in seats" rule in person. This turns passive recipients of design into active contributors.
- **The person who sketches directs the conversation.** Handing a pen to a colleague and asking them to sketch what they have in mind gives you direct insight into their mental model.
- **For remote teams:** Use Miro, FigJam, Mural, or Scribblar for shared visual collaboration. Scan sketches and distribute by email before the meeting. Sketch in real time via tablet during a shared screen session.

**"If You Only Do One Thing" (from the book):** Sketch. And while digital tools are common, the whiteboard variety accomplishes more in less time. Going analog is your secret weapon for getting people involved, excited, and engaged.

## Method 16: Wireframes

### What it is and when to use it

A wireframe is a skeletal depiction of what a product should look like -- a black-and-white schematic of all the pieces and parts of a product, screen by screen. It focuses on interaction, hierarchy, and content: what information appears on a screen, how it is laid out, what navigation is available, and how the user moves from one step to the next. Wireframes also include notes and annotations about product behavior -- making them a bridge between a design document and technical specifications.

Use it when you are ready to nail down the details of the product design.

**Average time:** Hours to weeks, depending on system scope. Each screen typically takes 30 minutes to a couple of hours.

### Step-by-step how-to

1. **Pick your tool.** Options include Axure RP, Figma, Sketch, and Balsamiq. Consider tool compatibility: who will need to edit these, do they need to be cloud-accessible, and does the tool integrate with your team's other systems (e.g., Jira, GitLab)?
2. **Create a template.** Choose or create a template for the specific device and screen resolution you are designing for (smartphone, laptop, desktop). Include spots for a page/screen name and for notes/annotations. Use a grid to organize layout.
3. **Create a wireframe inventory.** List all the screens you expect to create. Gather your sketches. For any screen without a sketch yet, sketch it on paper *before* going digital. Resolve functional questions in sketches, not in the wireframing tool.
4. **Get wireframing.** For each screen, consider:
   - *Sequence and state:* How does a user get here and where do they go next? Does this screen have multiple states based on different paths?
   - *Information density and priority:* What is the main message or action? Is the most important thing also the most visually obvious? Does the user need all of this at once?
   - *Grid:* How clean and balanced does the layout feel? Where could it fall apart at different breakpoints?
   - *Design principles:* Does this screen deliver on the experience described in the design brief's principles?
   - *Error states:* What does the user see when things go wrong?
5. **Step away periodically** and return with fresh eyes. Ask: "How can I make it even simpler?"
6. **Seek and listen to feedback.** Share wireframes with the team. Ask: do the designs make sense? Does the flow match how people expect to move through the product? What would they expect to happen when interacting with specific elements? Accept all feedback as valuable input.

### Inputs and outputs

- **Inputs:** Sketches, design brief, task flows, wireframe inventory list, device specifications.
- **Outputs:** Black-and-white screen-by-screen schematics with annotations, covering information structure, navigation, content hierarchy, interaction states, and error states. Serves as the basis for developer handoff and technical spec.

### Real example (from the book)

A low-fidelity wireframe of the Spotify app is offered as an example -- black-and-white, showing layout, navigation, and content hierarchy without any visual design attributes. It demonstrates that wireframes focus on *structure*, not aesthetics.

### Pitfalls and tips

- **Show your work early and often.** Never go far down the wireframing rabbit hole before checking in with colleagues. For a solo UX practitioner, showing work opens the black box of design and builds your colleagues' understanding of the process.
- **Don't skip sketching first.** Going digital too early triggers the impulse to make things pretty -- which distracts from functional decisions. Resolve those on paper.
- **Apply a grid.** Grid systems (column, baseline, or square) are mandatory for responsive design and ensure consistency and visual hierarchy across screen sizes.
- **Expect the "design triage" period.** Midway through wireframing, when designs move from ambiguous sketches to definite digital form, colleagues often suddenly express big concerns. This is normal. Plan for it. If your team is remote, try to be on-site and in person during this period.
- **Check tool compatibility early.** Figma integrates broadly (Jira, GitLab, etc.); Balsamiq works as a desktop app, cloud service, or plugin for Google Drive/Confluence/Jira; Sketch is macOS-only and not cloud-based, making collaboration harder.

## Best Practices

- Design is an exploratory, inclusive process -- invite people in and shepherd them through it.
- The design process is messy (the "squiggle") before it becomes clean. Structured, facilitative activities make colleagues partners rather than critics.
- Move from low-fidelity to high-fidelity progressively: design brief -> task flows -> sketches -> wireframes. Each step builds on the last and makes the next one faster and smarter.
- Cheap, rough work early (sketching) prevents expensive rework late (wireframes, code).
- Design principles from the brief should be checked against every artifact -- if a wireframe does not feel like the principles, something needs to change.
- Analog (whiteboard, pen, paper) consistently outperforms digital tools for collaborative exploration. Reserve digital tools for when you need to nail down the details.
- For a team of one, bringing colleagues into the design process is not just nice-to-have -- it is the primary quality-assurance mechanism and the source of political support for UX decisions.

## Real-World Application

**When a user asks you to help design a feature or screen:**
1. Before touching layout, ask what the design brief says -- or draft one together by running through the seven brief questions (focus, audience, features, feeling, uniqueness, principles, restrictions/expectations). This prevents wasted work.
2. Map the task flow for the feature first (even informally in text or a simple list of steps). Identify the "moment of truth" -- the most important destination -- and work outward from there.
3. Describe sketched alternatives in words (since you cannot draw), proposing 2--3 distinct approaches before recommending one. Explain the trade-offs.
4. When moving to wireframe-level detail, work through the wireframing checklist (sequence/state, information density, grid, design principles, error states) screen by screen.

**When a user shares existing designs for feedback:**
- Check them against the design brief questions: Does the screen communicate a clear primary action? Does it feel like the product's stated principles? Are error states accounted for?
- Flag any transition points between screens where the flow might break -- these are where UX most often fails.
- Ask whether the wireframe has been reviewed with engineers for feasibility.
