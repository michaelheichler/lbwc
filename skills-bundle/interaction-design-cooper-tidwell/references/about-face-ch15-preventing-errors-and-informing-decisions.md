# Preventing Errors and Informing Decisions

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 15.

## In one line

Replace error dialogs and blame-the-user messaging with three strategies instead: constant modeless status feedback, generous reversibility (Undo, Redo, versioning), and the ability to preview outcomes before committing to them.

## Core ideas

- Old-school interfaces relied on dialogs to explain what the user did wrong. Faster hardware removed most "the system can't do this" dialogs, and good design is now removing the "you made a mistake" dialogs too. The chapter's thesis: prevent errors and support decisions instead of reporting failures after the fact.
- Dialogs are a blunt communication channel. Subtle, continuous status information (especially positive status) almost never gets communicated because designers rightly avoid popping dialogs constantly. The fix is not less feedback, it's a different channel: modeless, visual, ambient.
- Rich Visual Modeless Feedback (RVMF): dense, always-on visual status baked into the interface itself (icon states, progress rings, color-coded diagrams, dynamic map icons) rather than requiring the user to open a dialog or check a status window. It works because the user absorbs status peripherally while doing other things, with no mode switch required to see it.
  - Why it works: it turns "ask the system" into "glance and know," and it scales, one screen can convey dozens of live attributes (see the Civilization game example: city advancement, unrest, unit health, territory lines, all shown without a word of text).
  - Caveat: RVMF is a skill users acquire over time, not something beginners instantly parse. Provide ToolTips or another discoverable explanation alongside it, and don't let RVMF be the only place critical warnings live until users have learned the vocabulary. Visually rank critical status above merely informational status so the important cues don't get lost among the decorative ones.
- Audible feedback follows the same logic as visual feedback, but the software world has trained users to associate sound with punishment (error beeps), so people say they hate sound in software. The actual problem is negative audible feedback, not audible feedback itself.
  - Negative audible feedback (a beep or noise on failure) mimics an alarm, and alarms are supposed to be rare, urgent, and jarring. Since users trigger "errors" constantly during normal use, the alarm framing is wrong for the frequency of the event, and it also implies success deserves no sound at all, which contradicts what people actually want (positive acknowledgment).
  - Positive audible feedback (a soft, pleasant sound on success) mirrors how the physical world already works: a door clicking when latched, a key clicking when typed, an engine turning over. Silence in these contexts already means "something's wrong," you don't need a scolding noise on top of it. Software should adopt the same pattern: quiet, pleasant confirmation sounds on success, and silence (not a beep) as the signal that something didn't register. This lets the system indicate a problem without ever insulting the user.
  - Sound must be user-controllable in volume and must not compete with other audio (media playback) on the device.
- Undo exists purely for humans. Computers don't make mistakes, so Undo has no basis in the implementation model, it must be modeled on the user's mental model instead, which is the chapter's central design instruction for the whole feature.
- Users rarely believe they are making a mistake in the moment. What looks like an "error" to the system (a false start, a paused action, an experimental probe) is normal human exploratory behavior. Designing the product as though every non-ideal input is a user error produces punitive, distrustful software. Undo should be framed as an exploration-support tool, not an error-confession tool, because that's how users actually experience their own actions.
- The psychological value of Undo is often more important than its mechanical value: knowing you can back out makes you willing to explore in the first place (the "rope ladder into the cave" idea). Users usually don't think about Undo until they suddenly need it, so it must be reliably present, not something added only where developers anticipated a mistake.
- Undo isn't goal-support, it's a trust-enabling safety net underneath the user's real goals. Because different personas hold different mental models of what Undo "is" (a panic button, a data-recovery bin, an operation stack), a robust Undo design should accommodate several of these models at once rather than assuming one.
- Undo should be a single, global, app-wide mechanism, working the same whether the last action came from direct manipulation or a dialog. Two frequent failures break user trust in Undo: losing Undo history after a save (there's no technical reason large modern storage can't preserve it), and Undo scope breaking down at the boundary of embedded objects (undo in an embedded spreadsheet doesn't necessarily match the user's single-document mental model of the host document).
- All the specific Undo variants below exist because a single naive mechanism (reverse the last function, strictly, one step at a time) increasingly diverges from what users actually want as situations grow more complex. The failure mode to watch for is literal behavior over practical behavior, i.e. an Undo that technically does what it says but throws away something valuable (six deleted paragraphs) to restore something trivial (one deleted word) purely because of LIFO ordering.
- Redo exists only because Undo enforces strict reverse-sequence order. It's a safety net for over-clicking Undo, letting the user step back forward without penalty.
- Group multiple Undo (as in Microsoft Word/Adobe apps) reveals a mismatch between the app's model and the user's model: selecting an item in the Undo history undoes everything back to that point as a bundle, not just that one item, and the product has to paper over the confusion with an explicit text label ("Undo 5 actions") because the underlying idiom fights the user's instinct to pick one thing off a list.
- Discontiguous, category-specific, and buffer-based Undo variants exist because real editing work isn't a clean linear stack, users often want to reverse one class of change (formatting) while keeping another (content), or want the deleted material itself rather than a literal step-by-step reversal.
- Versioning is a coarser-grained safety net than Undo: it snapshots the whole document at explicit user-requested checkpoints rather than tracking every incremental action. It suits situations where users want to back up a long distance without caring about the individual steps that got them there.
- Freezing locks down what has already been committed (like fixative on a drawing) while still allowing new additions, useful in creative or collaborative tools where you want to protect finished work without blocking new work.
- Some actions genuinely can't be undone because they trigger something outside the app's control (a sent email) or are constrained by policy or audit requirements (financial or medical records). In the truly-irreversible case, the design answer is not to give up on supporting the user's goal, it's to shift from "undo" to "a short grace window before commit" (Gmail's delayed send) or "a reversible adjustment plus an audit trail" so the user still gets a safety net appropriate to the constraint.
- Undo/Redo toggling is functionally a comparison tool already, flipping back and forth between two states is exactly what a "what-if" or preview feature does, it's just been left in its raw implementation form instead of being surfaced as a proper compare/preview control.

## Named patterns and principles

- **Rich Visual Modeless Feedback (RVMF)**: dense, idiomatic use of screen pixels to always display live status of objects/processes with no dialog or mode switch needed. Use for any attribute users need to monitor peripherally (device status, download progress, object state). Works because it lets users absorb information without interrupting their task, but needs supporting ToolTips or detail views since it's a learned visual language, and critical states need higher visual priority than routine ones.
- **Negative audible feedback**: a sound triggered on failure or error. Named as an anti-pattern: it borrows the "home alarm" framing (loud, rare, urgent) for events that are actually frequent and non-catastrophic, and it implies silence should mean success, which is backward from what people want.
- **Positive audible feedback**: a subtle, pleasant sound confirming a successful action (keystroke click, save chime, valid-drop tone), with silence (not a beep) signaling that something went wrong. Mirrors real-world objects (latching doors, humming appliances) where absence of sound is itself the failure signal. Avoids insulting the user while still communicating status.
- **Undo**: a facility that exists purely for human users, computers have no need of it, so it must be designed around the user's mental model of "what I just did," not the code's model of discrete function calls.
- **Blind Undo vs. Explanatory Undo**: blind Undo reverses the last action with no description of what that action was, explanatory Undo names the specific operation being reversed (e.g. "Undo Typing design"). Use explanatory Undo wherever feasible (menu items easily support it, toolbars can use a ToolTip) because naming the action builds user confidence in what's about to happen.
- **Single Undo**: reverses only the single most recent action. Simple, predictable, and often sufficient, but fails when the user doesn't notice a mistake immediately and takes further unrelated actions afterward, in which case a literal single-step reversal discards the wrong thing.
- **Multiple Undo (reversible history)**: a stack of past actions that can be undone repeatedly in reverse order. Solves the "mistake noticed late" problem of single Undo, but forces the user to also undo everything they did in between, even valid work, which can be its own cost, particularly if the intervening action was itself complex.
- **Redo**: reverses an Undo, letting the user step forward again after over-undoing. Necessary because strict LIFO Undo makes it easy to click one time too many.
- **Group multiple Undo**: the common commercial implementation (Word, Adobe apps) where selecting an item partway down the Undo history undoes that item and everything after it as one group, rather than that item alone. Named as a case where the represented model still doesn't match the user's actual mental model (users expect to pick a single past action), evidenced by the app needing an explicit text label to explain its own behavior.
- **Discontiguous multiple Undo**: lets the user reverse a non-adjacent subset of past actions while keeping others in between, closer to "select from a menu of past actions" than "pop a stack." Needed when the user took several interlaced actions and only some are undesirable, but it demands a richer, more explanatory presentation of the history because the ordering is no longer simply sequential.
- **Category-specific Undo**: separate Undo tracks per category of operation (e.g. a format-only Undo distinct from a content Undo, or per-tool Undo in a drawing app for pigment application vs. transformations vs. cut-and-paste). Modeled on how Backspace already works as a special, content-only, cursor-anchored Undo that users find natural despite being logically "discontiguous." Useful anywhere users mentally separate categories of change and want to reverse one without disturbing the other.
- **Deleted data buffer**: a separate repository of everything the user has deleted, independent of the function-based Undo stack, browsable and searchable directly (with timestamps and document names) rather than requiring the user to walk backward through every intervening step to retrieve one deleted item. Complements, doesn't replace, incremental Undo, since the data is already being retained anyway.
- **Versioning and reversion**: explicit, user-requested full-document snapshots that let the user revert to an entire earlier state without tracking individual actions. Best when the user wants to back up a long distance and doesn't care about the granular path that got them there. A reversion should itself create a new version so nothing is destructively lost, and the version list should carry enough metadata (time, author, size, notes) for the user to choose confidently.
- **Freezing**: locking already-entered data as immutable while still allowing new data to be added on top, like fixative on a drawing. Suited to creative or layered documents where you want to protect completed work from accidental disturbance while continuing to build on it.
- **Compare / What-if / Preview**: surfacing the comparison behavior implicit in toggling Undo/Redo as its own first-class control, letting the user view two states (with vs. without a change) side by side or in quick succession before committing. Named as an underused idiom: current tools usually leave this power buried in the raw Undo/Redo toggle instead of exposing it directly (thumbnail previews of filters or effects are the closest common realization).

## How to apply

- Wherever a dialog would only report a status change, check if that state can be shown continuously and modelessly instead (an icon, a color, a badge, a progress ring) so users absorb it without an interruption.
- Give critical or urgent status visual priority over decorative or purely informational status so learners can find the important cues first.
- Pair any nontrivial ambient visual cue with a discoverable explanation (ToolTip or equivalent), don't assume the visual language is self-evident to new users.
- Add subtle positive-confirmation sounds for successful actions where audio is available and appropriate, and let silence (never a beep) signal that an input wasn't accepted. Make volume controllable and don't fight with concurrent audio.
- Design Undo before deciding what counts as an "error." Treat every user action as valid from the user's point of view, and build Undo as a way to support exploration and change of mind, not as a confession mechanism.
- Make Undo global and consistent across the whole app regardless of whether the triggering action was direct manipulation or a dialog-driven command.
- Preserve Undo history across saves if storage allows it, saving work shouldn't force the user to commit to every prior action.
- Prefer explanatory Undo (name the action being reversed) over blind Undo wherever there's room to show it.
- For any product where users iterate on non-text content (images, layouts, effects), consider surfacing a dedicated Compare or Preview control instead of leaving comparison buried in raw Undo/Redo toggling.
- When a true Undo is impossible for policy or technical reasons (financial records, sent messages), don't drop the safety net entirely, offer a delay-before-commit window or a reversible adjustment plus audit trail instead.
- Before shipping any feature that destroys or transforms user data, ask whether it should be reversible, and if it should not be reversible, ask why not and whether that reasoning would satisfy the user, not just the implementation.

## Watch out for

- Don't build Undo that behaves literally correct but practically wrong, undoing the last, smallest action even when a much more valuable prior action is what the user actually wants back (the "keep the quarter, lose the fifty" trap).
- Don't let embedded objects (a spreadsheet inside a document) fracture the user's single-document mental model of Undo, where invoking Undo unexpectedly reverses the wrong sub-editor's last action.
- Watch for UI that has to explain its own inconsistency with a text label (e.g. "Undo 5 actions") as a sign the underlying model still doesn't match the user's mental model, adding a label is a patch, not a fix.
- Negative audible feedback (error beeps) trains users to hate sound in software generally, don't reintroduce it even for "important" errors, find a positive or silent framing instead.
- Don't assume RVMF alone is sufficient for beginners or for critical failure states, back it with discoverable detail and don't let ambient status be the only place a serious warning lives until users have learned to read it.
- Category-specific and discontiguous Undo variants are powerful but can be genuinely confusing if the ordering or scope isn't visually obvious to the user (unlike Backspace, whose scope is visually self-evident because of the cursor). Don't add this complexity without a clear, visible representation of what will be undone.
