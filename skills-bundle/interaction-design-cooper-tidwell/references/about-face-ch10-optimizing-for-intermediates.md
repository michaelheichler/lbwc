# Optimizing for Intermediates

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 10.

## In one line

Design mainly for the perpetual intermediate, the user who is neither a first-time beginner nor a master expert, because that is where almost everyone lives almost all the time.

## Core ideas

- Skill distribution across any user population forms a bell curve. Few true beginners, few true experts, a large hump of intermediates in the middle. Products built by engineers tend to skew toward expert assumptions (every feature looks equally important to the people who built and debugged all of them), while products shaped by marketing tend to skew toward beginner assumptions (marketing mostly watches first-time demos). Both biases fail the actual majority.
- Beginner status is temporary by nature. People dislike feeling incompetent, so they either climb quickly into intermediate competence or they abandon the product. Nobody aims to stay a beginner, so designing primarily for that state optimizes for a population that is already leaving.
- Expertise is also a state people cycle through, not a stable resting point. Skills fade when a product goes unused for weeks or months. A returning user is not a beginner (they retain concepts and relationships) but is rusty on specifics (they forget which command does what). Cooper calls this population "perpetual intermediates": always adequate, striving for fluency, ebbing and flowing with frequency of use, rarely graduating all the way to expert and rarely regressing to true beginner either. This is distinct from Larry Constantine's earlier term "improving intermediates," which implied steady forward progress toward expertise. Cooper's point is that most people plateau in the middle instead.
- Splitting the interface into a beginner mode (wizards) and an expert mode (deep menus) fails the majority twice over. It forces intermediates through beginner hand-holding they have outgrown, or it drops them into expert-only complexity they are not ready for. Neither wizards nor buried menus serve the middle well.
- The fix is to organize (inflect) the interface around actual usage patterns rather than around a beginner/expert split. Put what people use constantly in the most reachable spots, push what people use rarely deeper, and support graceful ramp-up and fallback for everyone else.
- People will tolerate a harder path only when the payoff is worth it to them personally, not when the feature is objectively impressive or effortful to build. This means UI complexity has to be earned by outcome value, not distributed evenly across all features just because engineering effort was even.
- A returning intermediate does not need to be re-taught scope and purpose (they already absorbed the mental model). They need quick memory joggers for specifics they let slip. That is a different, lighter kind of help than what a true beginner needs.
- Each experience tier asks structurally different questions of the product. Beginners ask what it does, what it does not do, and how to get started. Intermediates ask how to find or recall something they half-remember and how to undo a mistake. Experts ask about automation, shortcuts, customization, and what could go wrong. Designing help, labels, and defaults around only one of those question sets shortchanges the other two.
- Some product categories genuinely are beginner-optimized by necessity, transient-use tools (many mobile apps), attention-divided contexts (heads-up displays), public kiosks (ATMs, museum displays), and devices used by people with impairments (glucometers used by patients with reduced finger sensation and vision issues). These need to default to maximal clarity every single time because users rarely build up fluency at all.
- Other categories are legitimately expert-optimized, professional tools people use for a large share of their working hours (dev tools, creative authoring, scientific instruments, professional medical devices). Users of these arrive already trained and expect density and power, not hand-holding.
- Experts carry outsized influence on how a product is perceived, because prospects trust expert opinion during evaluation. But an expert's "this is bad" often means "this doesn't serve expert needs," which is a different claim than "this fails typical users." Do not let expert feedback alone override design decisions aimed at intermediates.
- Standard reference-style online help fits perpetual intermediates well (they know what they are looking for and search or use the index) but does not fit beginners well. Beginners do not yet know what to search for, they need overview and orientation instead of reference lookup.

## Named patterns and principles

- **Commensurate effort**: users will invest more work in a feature only in proportion to how much they personally value the result, regardless of how hard that feature was to build. Use it to decide how much interface complexity a feature is allowed to demand. Complexity is acceptable for producing complex, high-value results, but it is not acceptable in the path to a simple, everyday result.
- **Progressive disclosure**: hide advanced or infrequently used controls behind an expandable toggle (a "sticky" expand/collapse pane) rather than deleting them or always showing them. Use it wherever a feature is valuable to some users but noise to most, so intermediates get a lightweight peek and experts get a persistent expanded state that survives across sessions.
- **Inflection of the interface**: organize the interface around real navigation patterns instead of a flat feature list, by pulling frequent actions to the surface and pushing rare ones deeper. Use it as the top-level layout strategy for any screen or menu system, driven by three factors:
  - Frequency of use: daily-use items belong within immediate reach (zero clicks), several-times-daily items within a click or two, everything else two or three clicks deep, and truly rare (but still valuable) items removed from the everyday workspace without being deleted from the product.
  - Degree of dislocation: how jarring or disruptive invoking a function is to the user's current view or document. The more disruptive, the deeper it should be tucked, so it is not triggered by accident.
  - Degree of risk exposure: how irreversible or dangerous a function's consequences are. The greater the consequence, the more deliberate the access path should be (Cooper's analogy: missile launch keys require two people acting together), to prevent accidental invocation.
- **Perpetual intermediates**: Cooper's replacement term for what Larry Constantine called "improving intermediates" in Software for Use (1999). The distinction matters. Constantine's phrase implies steady forward motion toward expertise, while Cooper argues most users plateau indefinitely in a competent-but-not-expert middle state, cycling between rusty and refreshed rather than climbing a ladder.

## How to apply

- Default every general-purpose product's primary design target to the perpetual intermediate, not the first-run beginner and not the power user, unless the product category is one of the recognized exceptions (transient/attention-divided/public/impairment-affected contexts favor beginners, or professional/expert tools favor experts).
- Build in onboarding that teaches scope, purpose, and the core mental model quickly, then gets out of the way permanently. Never leave a mandatory tutorial, wizard, or persistent assistant character bolted onto the main workflow after the user has crossed the beginner threshold.
- Design any first-run guidance as an overview (what is this for, what can it do) rather than as reference material. Save reference-style, searchable help for the intermediate/expert population who already know the vocabulary and just need to look something up.
- Place the small, high-frequency, high-value working set of controls at zero navigation cost. Use progressive disclosure for the next tier (useful but not constant). Push rare, disruptive, or risky functions further away, and gate genuinely dangerous actions behind deliberate, hard-to-trigger interactions.
- When adding an advanced or complex feature, ask whether its value to the target persona justifies the interface cost it adds. If the answer is no for most personas, keep it out of the primary flow even if it is technically easy to expose.
- Provide shortcuts and customization paths for the features an intermediate uses often enough to want faster access, and expect expert users to want shortcuts to nearly everything they touch regularly.
- Keep some visible signal that deeper, more powerful capability exists (an accessible-but-not-intrusive advanced panel, a visible-but-collapsed settings area) even for users who will never open it. That visibility itself reassures perpetual intermediates that the product has headroom.
- Treat platform-transient or low-frequency-use contexts (a rarely opened mobile app, a public kiosk, a one-off form) as beginner-first design problems regardless of how sophisticated the underlying feature set is, since users there will not accumulate the repetition needed to become intermediate.

## Watch out for

- Do not build separate beginner and expert modes (wizard-for-novices plus buried-menu-for-experts) as a substitute for real inflection. This pattern strands intermediates, too slow through the wizard, too obscure in the raw menus, with nothing built for the middle.
- Do not treat expert critique as representative of majority user needs. An expert's complaint about missing depth or friction may be valid only for expert workflows and wrong for the intermediate majority.
- Do not rely on standard reference-style help documentation to onboard true beginners. Reference material assumes the user already knows what to search for, and beginners need overview and guided orientation instead.
- Do not distribute UI prominence evenly across features just because they took equal engineering effort to build. Prominence should track user-perceived value and frequency of use, not implementation cost.
- Do not permanently graft "helpful" interruption mechanisms (Cooper cites Microsoft's Clippy as the canonical failure) onto the core experience. Experts ignore them, beginners quickly resent them as reminders of incompetence, and perpetual intermediates end up stuck enduring them indefinitely.
- Do not assume all products should optimize for intermediates. Confirm whether the product's actual usage pattern makes it an exception (beginner-only context, or professional expert-only tool) before applying the default.
