# Metaphors, Idioms, and Affordances

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 13.

## In one line

Stop building interfaces around real-world metaphors and instead design learnable idioms with clear affordances, since metaphors are hard to find, don't scale, and cap your software at the limits of physical objects, while idioms only need to be learned once and can express anything software can do.

## Core ideas

- Interfaces evolved through three paradigms in order: implementation-centric (mirrors internal code structure), metaphoric (mirrors physical-world objects), and idiomatic (based on learned conventions). Modern content-centric UIs are mostly idiomatic. Know which paradigm you're defaulting to and move up the ladder.
- Implementation-centric UI is the easiest to build (one control per function, matching internal data structures) because engineers like exposing how things work, but it forces users to understand the system's internals just to operate it. Most users would rather succeed at a task than understand the mechanism behind it.
- A close cousin is the "org-chart-centric" interface: a product or website organized by which internal team or division owns the content rather than by how users think about the task. It fails for the same reason, it demands the user learn your org chart to find anything.
- Metaphoric UI borrows recognizable pictures from the physical world (a trash can, a checkbook) so users can transfer prior knowledge. It works because of intuition, not magic: intuition is inference from prior learned experience, distinct from instinct (hardwired reactions needing no learning, like flinching at a loud noise). A wastebasket icon is understood because the user already learned real wastebaskets, not because software is inherently intuitive.
- Metaphors break down badly at scale and under extension. Problems:
  - Good metaphors are scarce and don't scale (file icons worked for a floppy disk of files, not for a terabyte drive with tens of thousands of files).
  - Most software processes (purchasing, filtering, statistical analysis, changing settings) have no natural physical-world counterpart to borrow a metaphor from.
  - Metaphors depend on shared cultural background between designer and user, and fail or mislead across cultures or even within one (does a plane icon mean "check arrivals" or "book a flight"?).
  - A metaphor helps first-time users the most, then becomes a tax: it nails the product's conceptual model to the ground and blocks it from exceeding what the physical original could do.
- The worst failure mode is the "global metaphor," a single overarching metaphor forced onto an entire product (e.g. Magic Cap's virtual desk, hallway, and street). Once one metaphor is chosen, the temptation to keep extending it to related interactions is nearly irresistible, and each extension adds real navigation overhead without any real-world benefit. Software has no obligation to preserve the friction of physical objects, so recreating that friction is a pure loss.
- The file-and-folder system illustrates hidden costs of metaphor extension: paper folders don't nest ten levels deep and a physical document can't live in two folders at once, but software has no such constraint, yet many systems inherit the constraint anyway purely because they inherited the metaphor.
- Metaphor still has legitimate uses: video games (diegetic interfaces keep players immersed), simulators (flight simulators intentionally replicate real controls to transfer trained skill), and creative/musical tools especially on touchscreens, where mimicking a physical instrument's controls can be genuinely expressive rather than just decorative. Use metaphor when the real-world interaction itself is the value being delivered, not as a learnability shortcut.
- Idiomatic design borrows from how humans learn figures of speech: an idiom like "kick the bucket" carries no logical or associative link to its meaning, you either learn it from context or are taught it, and once learned it's remembered easily and permanently. This is why idioms don't require literal or metaphorical grounding, and it's why they can represent any function, however abstract, since they aren't limited to things with real-world physical analogs.
- Most of what feels "intuitive" in graphical UI (windows, title bars, close boxes, drop-downs, even the mouse itself) is actually idiomatic, learned once through experience or brief instruction, not decoded via metaphor. The mouse has zero visual resemblance to its function, but the immediate visual feedback of cursor motion teaches the connection almost instantly. That fast, sticky, one-time learning is the signature of a good idiom.
- Good graphical vocabularies are built in layers, like language: primitives (indivisible actions: point, click, drag, keypress) combine into compounds (double-click, buttons, checkboxes, resize handles), which combine into domain-specific idioms (navigation bars, list boxes, application-specific dialogs). Keeping the atomic vocabulary small is what makes the whole system learnable. More atomic elements means longer learning time, but a small set of primitives can still compose into unlimited domain-specific complexity, just as letters compose into unlimited words and sentences.
- Manual affordance (Cooper's adaptation of Norman's "affordance") is a purely cognitive/perceptual property: what a user believes an object can do based on its shape, not what it actually does. A doorbell-shaped button "is" a doorbell affordance-wise even if wired to a trapdoor, because affordance lives in perception and prior learning (socialization with doorbells and buttons), reinforced by innate tool-manipulation instincts (round finger-sized things invite pushing, bar-shaped things invite gripping).
- A control's shape only signals that it can be manipulated, not what manipulating it will do. Unlike physical mechanisms, you can't trace a screen control's internal wiring by inspection, so software must supply that "what it does" information via labels, icons, or learned convention (a scrollbar's arrows hint at direction, but its function as a document-position control must be taught or discovered).
- Software makes an implicit contract with the user through its manual affordances: a rendered button must visibly react when actuated and must actually do what its label promises. Real-world tools can't lie about their affordances (a saw looks like exactly what it does), but software can present false affordances (bait-and-switch controls), which breaks user trust, so an agent should never render a control that looks pliant/actionable but isn't, or that performs something other than what its labeling implies.
- Direct manipulation (Shneiderman, 1974) is the dominant modern interaction strategy, built from three elements: visual representation of the actual data objects, visible/gestural ways to act on them directly (instead of typed commands), and immediately visible results of the action. The visual-feedback half of this is arguably the most important part. An interaction without adequate visual feedback fails to feel like direct manipulation even if the underlying mechanic is the same.
- Direct manipulation is powerful for spatial/qualitative tasks (dragging a tab marker, reordering a list by feel, sketching a waveform, drag-and-drop into a queue) because it lets a user express intent that no algorithm can infer, like personal preference ordering. It's a poor fit for precise numeric entry (nobody sketches "1.347 inches" easily), and it demands motor skill and dexterity from the user, so it can create difficulty for complex or precision tasks (e.g. CAD-like manipulation) even though it's easy for casual movement tasks. Balance how much manipulation you delegate to the user against their skill level and how well an algorithm could just do it for them.
- Pliancy is whether an object or region responds to user input and can be manipulated (a button, a spreadsheet cell, a draggable object are all pliant). Pliancy should almost always be visually signaled. The one exception is a dense expert-only tool where screen space for learnability cues is a worse tradeoff than density, and even then that tradeoff should be made deliberately, not by default.
- There are four ways to hint at pliancy, matched to context:
  - Static hinting bakes the affordance into the object's constant rendering (a 3D-looking button). Works well on mobile, where fewer, larger objects fit comfortably, but overused on dense desktop screens it turns the UI into a cluttered "sculpture garden" of fake dimensionality.
  - Dynamic hinting changes an object's appearance only on cursor flyover/rollover, before any click. It keeps a clean baseline UI (toolbar icons look flat until hovered) while still surfacing pliancy exactly when relevant, and it has no real equivalent on touch devices since there's no hover state.
  - Pliant response hinting is the feedback during the press itself, before release: a button visibly indents while held, and springs back without activating if the user drags off it before releasing. This is what makes press-and-drag-away-to-cancel work, and it's commonly missed by custom-built controls.
  - Cursor hinting changes the cursor icon itself when it passes over a pliant region (e.g. a resize double-arrow at a window edge, or Excel's column-divider drag cursor). It's the right tool when the object is too small or too data-dense to visually decorate without harming its content (tabular data, thin dividers), and like dynamic hinting it has no touchscreen equivalent.
- The recent (as of writing) trend toward flat, minimal visual design strips away the skeuomorphic shading/shadows that used to double as static affordance cues. This makes flat UIs visually simpler but often harder to learn, because the "push me" and "drag me" signals that used to be free (via 3D-looking rendering) now have to be deliberately re-solved with typography, color, or motion, or they're lost.

## Named patterns and principles

- **Manual affordance**: Cooper's redefinition of Norman's "affordance," dropping "actual" so it becomes purely about perceived, learned, and instinctive cues to how an object can be physically manipulated (pushed, pulled, gripped, dragged), regardless of what it truly does underneath. Use it to judge whether a control's shape correctly signals its interactivity, independent of whether its behavior matches.
- **Global metaphor**: a single overarching real-world metaphor imposed on an entire product's navigation and structure (e.g., Magic Cap's desk/hallway/street). Avoid it outside of games/simulators, since consistency with the metaphor becomes more important than efficiency, and every interaction inherits the physical world's friction for no benefit.
- **Idiomatic design ("design of principles," per Ted Nelson)**: building UI from learned conventions rather than intuited metaphors or exposed implementation. Use this as the default paradigm for nearly all modern software, since idioms need no real-world parallel and only need to be learned once.
- **Interaction vocabulary pyramid (primitives, compounds, idioms)**: a three-layer model for building a learnable UI language: atomic indivisible actions at the base (point, click, drag, tap), simple combined objects/actions in the middle (buttons, double-click, checkboxes), and full domain-specific idioms on top (navigation bars, dialogs, app-specific commands). Use it to check that a design keeps its atomic vocabulary small while still supporting rich, specific top-level idioms.
- **Direct manipulation (Shneiderman, 1974)**: an interaction strategy defined by three parts, visual representation of data objects, visible/gestural direct action on them, and immediate visible results. Use it for tasks where the user's intent is best expressed spatially or by feel (reordering, sketching, positioning), and treat the visual-feedback element as non-negotiable. Without it the interaction doesn't feel direct even if mechanically similar.
- **Pliancy**: the property of an object or region that responds to and can be acted on by user input. Use it as the concept underlying every hinting decision, first establish that something is pliant, then choose how to signal it.
- **Static, Dynamic, Pliant-response, and Cursor hinting**: the four techniques for visually signaling pliancy (baked-in appearance, hover-triggered change, press-state feedback, and cursor-icon change). Choose based on platform (mobile favors static, since hover doesn't exist, desktop can use all four) and density (cursor hinting for small/data-dense controls that can't absorb decoration).

## How to apply

- Default to idiomatic design. Only reach for a metaphor when the real-world interaction itself is the point (games, simulators, expressive creative tools on touch), never as a learnability shortcut.
- Never let a metaphor constrain a feature the software could otherwise do better than its physical counterpart (arbitrary folder nesting, multi-location filing, multi-way linking). If you catch yourself limiting a digital object to match its physical namesake's constraints, stop and ask why.
- Keep the atomic interaction vocabulary small (a handful of primitives: point, click, drag, tap, swipe) and build all complexity by composing those primitives into compounds and then domain idioms, don't invent new atomic gestures per feature.
- Give every pliant control a legible label or icon that states what it does, not just that it's clickable. Shape alone can only signal "this reacts," never "this does X."
- Honor the affordance contract: if a control looks pushable/draggable, make sure pressing or dragging it does something coherent with its appearance and label. Never ship a control that looks actionable but is inert, or that does something unrelated to what it visually promises.
- Choose a pliancy-hinting technique deliberately per platform: static hinting for mobile (fewer, larger objects), dynamic/rollover hinting for desktop toolbars and dense screens, pliant-response feedback for every press-and-hold or press-and-drag-away-to-cancel interaction, cursor hinting for small or data-dense controls (dividers, resize handles, thin controls embedded in tables).
- When designing flat/minimal visual style, explicitly compensate for the loss of skeuomorphic affordance cues, decide how pliancy will be communicated (typography, color, spacing, motion) rather than letting flatness silently erase learnability.
- Reserve direct manipulation for tasks where the user's felt sense of "where" or "how much" matters (ordering, positioning, freehand shaping) and route precise numeric entry to a text field or slider instead. Watch the skill and context of your personas before leaning heavily on direct manipulation for complex tasks.
- When organizing a website or app's information architecture, structure it around how users think about their tasks, not around your internal team/division/module boundaries (avoid org-chart-centric structure).

## Watch out for

- Don't assume "intuitive" means magical. It always traces back to either instinct (rare, hardwired) or intuition (inference from something the user already learned), so if there's no learnable connection available to your users, the interface won't feel intuitive to them no matter how obvious it seems to you.
- Watch for well-meaning extension of a metaphor past simple function recognition, e.g., a phone metaphor that also forces dialing by number pad when the software could let users just tap a photo of a contact. Extension for its own sake reintroduces physical-world limits with no offsetting benefit.
- Cross-cultural and cross-experience failure is a real risk for any metaphor-based icon or interaction, since it depends on the designer and user sharing background associations. An idiom sidesteps this but must then be explicitly taught or made discoverable.
- Bait-and-switch affordances (controls that look pliant/actionable but behave inconsistently with their appearance or labeling) are especially common on the web, where the distinction between controls, content, and decoration is often blurry, actively check for this.
- Overusing static 3D-style hinting on dense desktop screens creates visual clutter (the "sculpture garden" problem), prefer dynamic or cursor hinting there instead.
- Flat, minimal visual trends can quietly erase pliancy cues that used to come for free from skeuomorphic shading. This is a known tradeoff, not an oversight to ignore, budget explicit design effort to restore lost learnability signals.
- Touchscreen interfaces have no equivalent to dynamic hinting (hover) or cursor hinting, since there's no cursor and no hover state, so pliancy signaling on touch must rely entirely on static hinting and other strategies (motion, animation, discoverability aids covered elsewhere in the book).
- Direct manipulation is not automatically good for every task, complex or high-precision work (numeric entry, structured data manipulation) can leave users floundering if forced into manipulation gestures better suited to simple qualitative tasks. Evaluate against the persona's skill level and the task's actual precision needs before defaulting to it.
