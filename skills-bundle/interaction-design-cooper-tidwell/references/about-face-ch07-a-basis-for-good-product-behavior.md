# A Basis for Good Product Behavior

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 7, "A Basis for Good Product Behavior."

## In one line

Good product behavior rests on three layered tools, design values that state what design should be for, design principles that turn those values into general rules, and design patterns that turn those rules into reusable, context-specific solutions.

## Core ideas

- A design is not "good" just because it satisfies user goals and business constraints. Cooper argues you need an explicit value system underneath the process, otherwise every tradeoff decision is made ad hoc. Values, principles, and patterns are three levels of the same idea, values are beliefs, principles are rules derived from those beliefs, patterns are proven solutions that embody the rules.
- Design values exist because interactive products are not passive communication, they act on the world. A poster persuades, a product executes policy or performs work directly. That means designers carry more responsibility for downstream effects than graphic or brand designers do, and that responsibility has to be named explicitly rather than assumed.
- Four design values (developed by Reimann, Dubberly, Goodwin, Fore, and Korman) frame every downstream principle:
  - Ethical: considerate and helpful, do no harm and improve human situations.
  - Purposeful: useful and usable, help users reach their goals inside their real contexts and capacities.
  - Pragmatic: viable and feasible, help the commissioning organization succeed and respect business and technical constraints.
  - Elegant: efficient, artful, affective, the simplest complete solution with internal coherence that fits how people think and feel.
- "Do no harm" is deliberately broad. Cooper lists six harm categories (interpersonal, psychological, physical, economic, social/societal, environmental) precisely because the easy ones (interpersonal, psychological) are the ones designers naturally think about, while economic, societal, and environmental harm are second- or third-order effects that are easy to miss but can be more consequential (a stock-trading app, a voting system, an industrial control system). The point is to force a wider sweep of who else, besides the direct user, the product touches.
- Environmental harm is treated as a full lifecycle question, not just "is this product green." Cooper cites five lifecycle phases with an environmental footprint: manufacturing, transportation, usage/energy consumption, recyclability, and facilities. For most software work only usage/energy and facilities apply, but the reasoning still holds, a design decision that increases data usage or server load has a footprint even if no physical object changed hands.
- Ethics is not just harm avoidance. A design should also actively improve situations, increasing understanding, increasing individual/group efficiency, improving communication, reducing sociocultural tension, improving equity, balancing diversity with cohesion. The reasoning is that "not bad" is a low bar, designers should look for the affirmative opportunity even when it is outside the immediate brief.
- "Purposeful" reframes the whole Goal-Directed process as an ethical stance, not just a method. Understanding user goals is necessary but not sufficient, you also have to understand user limitations (weaknesses, blind spots) so the product supports people where they are weak and amplifies them where they are strong. This is why persona-based research pays off, it surfaces both sides.
- "Pragmatic" is a warning against treating design as separate from business and engineering reality. A design that never gets built, funded, or shipped has zero value regardless of quality. But pragmatism cuts both ways, developers who say a design is "impossible" often mean "impossible on the current schedule," and marketing plans built on aggregate statistics can miss how individual users actually behave. Designers who have done real qualitative research sometimes see the business model more clearly than the business side does. The fix is not designer deference or designer stubbornness, it is mutual trust and open dialog among design, business, and engineering about which constraints are truly fixed.
- "Elegant" splits into two ideas: economy of form (do more with less) and internal coherence (the product feels like one unified thing, not a collection of screens bolted together). Economy of form is not decoration, minus, it is functional, fewer screens, fewer widgets, fewer visual distinctions, mapped tightly onto the user's mental model, because every extra element is something the user has to parse and remember. Internal coherence often fails not from bad taste but from process, separate teams building separate modules without talking, or hardware and software designed independently. The Goal-Directed process's top-down-then-refine sequence and its reliance on a single narrative scenario are the structural antidote, one story forces one coherent product.
- Elegance also means matching cognitive and emotional register to context, not chasing "desire" as a universal design emotion. Desire is the wrong target for a technical or high-stakes tool, a technician running a radiation therapy system should feel careful and attentive, not delighted. The generalizable rule: figure out what emotional and cognitive state actually serves the user's purpose in that context, and design toward that state rather than toward generic delight.
- Interaction design principles are the operational layer under values. They exist at three levels of granularity, and this leveling matters because it tells you where in the design process a given rule should be applied:
  - Conceptual principles, shape what the product fundamentally is and how it fits into the user's broader context of use.
  - Behavioral principles, shape how the product acts, in general and in specific situations.
  - Interface principles, shape how behavior and information get organized, navigated, and communicated on screen.
- Most behavioral and interface principles reduce to one underlying move, minimize the user's work while maximizing useful feedback and context. This is the default optimization target for productivity and utility products. Entertainment products are the deliberate exception, games succeed by asking for exactly the right amount of effort and then rewarding it (Cooper cites Farmville-style social games), so "minimize work" is not a universal law, it is the rule for tools and the anti-pattern for engagement-driven experiences. Too much work turns a game into a chore, but so does too little reward, or a clunky interface that blocks the "fun" work.
- Design patterns exist to capture and reuse solutions instead of re-deriving them project by project. Cooper's argument for investing in them: they cut design time, raise solution quality, improve designer-developer communication, and train new designers faster.
- Interaction design patterns descend from Christopher Alexander's architectural pattern language work, specifically because Alexander's goal was to name the structural features that produce a felt sense of well-being for the people inside a space, which is exactly what interaction designers want for the people inside a digital product. This lineage is why the patterns should be judged by lived, human effect and not just by formal reuse value, unlike engineering patterns, which mainly exist to reuse code.
- The key structural difference from architecture: interaction patterns are not static, they capture dynamic behavior, how the interface changes state in response to both the user's activity and the application's own state. That is different from a mechanical product or a film's preordained sequence, and it is different from a static building layout.
- A properly recorded pattern captures four things, not just a picture: the context it applies to, one or more concrete example solutions, the abstracted features common across the examples, and the rationale for why it works. Skipping the rationale is what turns a pattern into a cargo-culted template.
- Patterns are never plug-and-play. Jenifer Tidwell's caveat, quoted directly: "[Patterns] aren't off-the-shelf components, each implementation of a pattern differs a little from every other." Cooper reinforces this with Alexander's own point that architectural patterns are the opposite of prefab construction, the pattern's real form depends on its surrounding context and the other patterns that compose, contain, or sit next to it. Practically, this means a style guide can never substitute for a context-specific design decision, and even experienced designers should expect to adapt a pattern's specifics for each new domain while keeping the underlying object-to-object and object-to-goal relationships intact.
- A comprehensive pattern catalog does not turn novice designers into good designers by mechanical assembly. Even seasoned designers apply patterns with judgment about context, not by matching a catalog entry.

## Named patterns and principles

- **Design values (Ethical, Purposeful, Pragmatic, Elegant)**: the top-level belief system behind all design decisions. Use them as a checklist when evaluating any design tradeoff, not just at kickoff. They work because they force explicit consideration of harm, user goals, business viability, and simplicity as four separate lenses that catch different failure modes.
- **Do no harm / Improve human situations**: the two-part ethical mandate. Apply "do no harm" as a pre-mortem across the six harm categories (interpersonal, psychological, physical, economic, social, environmental) before shipping anything with real-world stakes. Apply "improve human situations" as an affirmative check, look for a way the product could make things measurably better, not merely non-harmful.
- **Economy of form**: the elegance principle that the simplest complete solution beats a more elaborate one. Use it when trimming screens, widgets, or visual distinctions. It works because every added element is cognitive load the user must carry, and because a smaller solution surface is easier to keep coherent.
- **Internal coherence**: the elegance principle that a product should read as one unified whole rather than assembled parts. Apply it by driving design top-down from a single concept and testing it against one continuous scenario narrative, rather than letting independent teams build disconnected modules. Works because a shared narrative thread is what actually unifies disparate screens and features.
- **Minimize work (behavioral/interface principle)**: the default optimization target for utility and productivity products, reduce user effort while increasing useful feedback and contextual information. Use it for any tool-like or task-oriented product. Does not apply as-is to entertainment products, where the right amount of effort plus reward is the actual goal.
- **Postural patterns**: conceptual-level patterns describing a product's overall stance toward the user, for example "transient" (used only briefly so a larger goal can be pursued elsewhere). Use when deciding how much of the user's attention and time a product should claim. Covered in depth in the posture chapter of this book.
- **Structural patterns**: patterns for arranging information and functional elements on screen (views, panes, groupings). Use when laying out a screen or app shell. Two named examples from this chapter:
  - **Organizer-Workspace** (seen in Microsoft Outlook): a left navigation/organizer pane plus a right workspace split into an overview pane and a detail pane. Use for full-screen desktop applications where users need to browse many objects, act on groups of them, and inspect one object's detail, all without opening extra windows. Works because it keeps browsing, batch action, and detail inspection simultaneously visible and mutually connected on one screen.
  - **Double Drawer** (pioneered by Facebook and Path): a main content view with a left drawer (primary navigation) and sometimes a right drawer (ancillary object list, such as friends), both revealed by swiping the main view aside. Use for mobile apps, especially where one-handed thumb operation matters. Works because swiping is a simple gross-motor gesture suited to touch, and it keeps navigation and ancillary lists out of the way until needed.
- **Behavioral patterns**: lower-level patterns describing specific interactions with functional or data elements, essentially widget-level behaviors. Use when designing how an individual control or component should respond to input.
- **Pattern language / pattern library or catalog**: a rigorously organized, sufficiently complete set of patterns for a domain is a pattern language, a less complete or less rigorous set is a pattern library or catalog. Cooper notes that digital product design likely moves too fast for a true stable pattern language to form, so treat any interaction design pattern collection as a living catalog, not a finished language.

## How to apply

- Before finalizing a design decision, check it against all four values (Ethical, Purposeful, Pragmatic, Elegant), not just usability or business fit alone.
- Run an explicit harm sweep (interpersonal, psychological, physical, economic, social, environmental) on any product with real-world stakes, including software-only products with second-order effects like server load or workforce impact.
- Look for an affirmative "improve human situations" opportunity in every project, do not stop at "not harmful."
- When researching users, capture both their goals and their limitations (weaknesses, blind spots), and design the product to shore up weak points while amplifying strengths.
- Treat business and engineering constraints as inputs to negotiate with mutual trust, not as fixed dictates to accept uncritically or as obstacles to ignore. Ask whether a stated "impossible" is, in fact, a schedule constraint.
- Default to the simplest complete solution, cut screens, widgets, and visual distinctions that do not carry meaning.
- Drive coherence top-down from one product concept and one continuous scenario, rather than letting features accumulate independently across teams or modules.
- Choose the emotional and cognitive register deliberately based on context, do not default to "delight" or "desire" for tools used in high-stakes, technical, or enterprise contexts.
- Default to minimizing user work and maximizing useful feedback for utility and productivity products, switch to a work and reward balance model for games and entertainment products.
- Apply principles at the right altitude: conceptual principles when defining what the product is, behavioral principles when defining how it acts, interface principles when defining layout, navigation, and communication.
- When reusing a design pattern, adapt it to the specific context, objects, and neighboring patterns rather than copying it verbatim. Record the context, example, abstracted features, and rationale so the pattern stays reusable and legible to other designers.
- Reach for the Organizer-Workspace pattern for full-screen desktop apps needing simultaneous browse, batch-act, and detail-inspect. Reach for the Double Drawer pattern for one-handed mobile apps needing quick access to navigation and an ancillary object list.

## Watch out for

- Do not treat pattern catalogs as plug-and-play components, Tidwell's own caveat is that every implementation of a pattern differs from every other. Mechanically assembling patterns without understanding context produces incoherent results even for experienced designers, let alone novices.
- Do not let a style guide substitute for a context-specific design decision, the pattern's real form depends on its surrounding environment and adjacent patterns.
- Do not fixate only on the emotion of desire as the elegance target, it is the wrong goal for high-stakes, technical, or enterprise products where focus, caution, or reverence is the correct emotional register.
- Do not assume "minimize work" is a universal principle, applying it to games or other engagement-driven products removes the friction that makes them enjoyable. Too little required effort, or too little reward for the effort, breaks the experience just as too much friction does.
- Do not stop your harm analysis at interpersonal and psychological harm just because those are easiest to see, economic, social, and environmental harms are often second- or third-order and easy to miss, but can be the most consequential.
- Do not assume internal coherence will emerge on its own from good individual screens, it usually fails at the process level, disconnected teams or independently designed hardware and software, so the fix is process (single narrative, top-down concept) not just visual polish.
