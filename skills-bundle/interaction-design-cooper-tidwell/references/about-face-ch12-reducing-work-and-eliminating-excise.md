# Reducing Work and Eliminating Excise

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 12.

## In one line

Every interaction costs users cognitive, memory, visual, or physical work, and the designer's job is to strip out any of that work that does not directly serve the user's goal (excise), because excise is the single biggest driver of dissatisfaction with digital products.

## Core ideas

- Users do four kinds of work whenever they use a product: cognitive work (understanding behaviors, text, structure), memory work (recalling commands, passwords, locations of things), visual work (finding items, decoding layout, distinguishing controls from decoration), and physical work (clicks, keystrokes, gestures, mode switches). Design should minimize all four while still letting users reach their goals.
- Any big task decomposes into smaller ones. Some of those smaller tasks move the user directly toward the goal (goal-directed tasks). Others exist only to satisfy the tool, the platform, or some outside party (excise tasks). The car analogy: opening the garage door and stopping at red lights don't get you to work faster, they serve the car and the traffic system, not your destination. Software has the same split, and implementation-model thinking (building the UI around how the system happens to work internally) tends to multiply excise instead of removing it.
- Excise is not absolute, it is contextual. A behavior that is pure excise for one persona (configuring window layout) is a goal-directed feature for a persona who genuinely needs it (comparing two apps side by side). Judge excise against your specific personas' goals, not against some universal list.
- Excise also varies by posture (see the flow/posture chapter). Transient-posture apps get more slack for onboarding/explanatory screen space because users need reminding how the app works. Sovereign-posture apps get none of that slack, because users live in them daily and any recurring tax compounds into real pain over time.
- Some things are excise almost everywhere: hardware/system-management busywork (network config, backups, permissions dialogs) that the software could handle itself. Treat demands for this kind of information as a design failure to fix, not a fact of life.
- Interrupting a user's flow state (see prior chapter) for no real reason is one of the worst forms of excise, because flow takes effort to enter and an unnecessary interruption (a modal dialog, a needless confirmation) destroys it for nothing.
- Skeuomorphism, borrowing the look and behavior of a pre-digital mechanical artifact, is sometimes legitimate (word-processor "tabs" genuinely do the same job as typewriter tabs) but usually becomes a liability once the metaphor's job is done: it wastes screen space, hides more powerful digital-native interactions, and traps designers into replicating the old artifact's limitations instead of asking what the digital medium actually makes possible.
- Old systems are simple, computing systems are usually hierarchical, and that mismatch is a recurring source of navigation excise. People's native mental model for storing miscellaneous stuff is flat piles/stacks grouped by a shared trait, not nested trees. Deep nested hierarchies (folder-in-folder-in-folder) are a developer's implementation-model comfort, not a user's.
- Mapping (borrowed from Donald Norman, The Design of Everyday Things) is the relationship between a control, what it acts on, and the resulting effect. Bad mapping forces the user to stop and consciously puzzle out that relationship every time, which is cognitive load and a source of real errors, not just annoyance. Mapping failures come in at least two flavors: spatial/physical (a knob's position doesn't suggest which burner it fires) and logical/semantic (a label like "Ascending/Descending" doesn't match how people mentally categorize dates as "recent" vs "old").
- When a product's foundational metaphor comes from a pre-digital tool, don't assume the old form factor is the answer just because it's familiar. Ask what constraints of paper, mechanics, or physical space forced that form, and whether the digital medium removes the constraint. A one-month-page calendar exists because paper pages are a fixed size, screens are not, so a continuously scrollable calendar with adjustable row/column sizes may serve users far better even though it looks unfamiliar at first.
- Users do adapt to an unfamiliar new representation readily, as long as the payoff is real. The risk is copying old habits out of comfort while gaining nothing (Apple's iOS 7 calendar scrolled continuously but still hard-broke at month boundaries and skipped drag-to-select multi-day ranges, so it kept the old limitation while adding new complexity).

## Named patterns and principles

- **Excise** (goal-directed tasks vs. excise tasks): work a user must do that doesn't advance their goal but instead serves the tool, the implementation, or an outside party. Use this as the master lens: for every interaction step, ask whether it is excise for this persona in this context, and if so, cut it or shrink it.
- **"Eliminate excise wherever possible."** The chapter's core directive. Apply it as a standing design review question on every screen and flow.
- **Navigational excise**: any action that moves the user to a different part of the interface or makes them hunt for objects/tools/data elsewhere, counted as excise almost by definition except in games where wayfinding is the point. It shows up at four levels: across windows/views/pages, across adjacent or tabbed panes, across tools/menus/palettes, and within displayed information (scrolling, linking, zooming/panning). Minimize the number of "places" and the friction of moving between them.
- **Navigational trauma**: the disorientation and "getting lost" state users hit when an interface has too many windows/views to track. Sovereign-posture apps avoid it by keeping the primary interaction in one main view with multiple panes rather than scattering it across separate windows.
- **Skeuomorphic excise**: excise caused by faithfully copying a pre-digital, mechanical representation into software after its usefulness as a learning aid has expired. Valid early (helps first-time users map the new to the familiar), a liability later (wastes space, blocks better digital-native interaction, teaches users to "manage the metaphor" instead of doing their task).
- **Modal excise**: excise from unnecessary flow-breaking interruptions, chiefly modal error messages and confirmation dialogs that stop the user cold for something the software should have handled or asked more usefully.
- **"Don't stop the proceedings with idiocy."** Named principle about modal excise: don't interrupt the user to state something unhelpful, blame them for a problem the software caused, or demand a decision with no information to decide on.
- **"Don't make users ask permission."** Named principle: don't force a user through a separate screen/mode just to request the ability to edit something they're already looking at.
- **"Allow input wherever you have output."** Companion principle: wherever a value is displayed, let the user edit it directly, in place, rather than routing them to a separate input screen. This matches users' actual mental model, which doesn't distinguish "the place I see the number" from "the place I change the number."
- **Stylistic excise**: excise from visual work, when heavy styling or decoration makes it hard to tell controls from ornamentation, or to know where to start reading. Visual style should support communication and legibility, not compete with it, especially in productivity contexts (games/entertainment can spend more of this budget deliberately).
- **Signposts**: persistent, recognizable objects (main window chrome, menu bars, toolbars, a site's top nav) that let users orient themselves the way sailors use shorelines or stars. Use when the interface has more than a trivial number of navigable places. Works because reliability breeds trust: unexpected changes to a signpost (menus especially) erode users' confidence that they know where things are.
- **Overviews**: persistent aids that orient users within the content rather than within the app (thumbnail navigators, breadcrumb trails, annotated scrollbars). Use for any content space large enough that users can lose their place inside it (long documents, big canvases, deep site hierarchies).
- **Mapping** (Donald Norman): the perceptible relationship between a control, the thing it acts on, and the result. **Natural mapping** (Norman's term) is spatial layout that makes that relationship self-evident, for example arranging stove knobs in the same spatial pattern as the burners they control, instead of a straight row that leaves the user guessing. Use natural mapping whenever a control's target isn't already obvious from label or convention, because ambiguous mapping forces users to relearn the relationship every single use and increases real-world error rates, not just friction.
- **Monocline grouping**: Cooper's term for the flat, single-level way most people naturally group and store miscellaneous physical things (one shelf, one set of labeled piles), as opposed to deep nested hierarchies. Related to Norman's **pile cabinet** concept (people file by loose piles, not nested folders). Use it as the represented model users interact with (flat groups/tags/search) even while the implementation underneath may still be a deep hierarchical data structure optimized for scale, because forcing the hierarchy itself into the UI conflicts with how people actually think about storage.
- **"Significant change must be significantly better."** Principle for deciding whether to break from a familiar old (often skeuomorphic) model: users will adapt to an unfamiliar new representation without much resistance, but only if it delivers a real, meaningfully large improvement, not a change made just to look modern or different.

## How to apply

- Before shipping any flow, walk it step by step and label each step goal-directed or excise, checked against the actual persona's stated goal, not an abstract "typical user."
- Count "places" (windows, dialogs, panes, tabs, pages) in a flow and try to reduce that count first, before polishing any individual place. Prefer one primary view with a few adjacent panes over routing users through separate windows or tabs, especially for sovereign-posture products used daily.
- Reserve tabs for content that is genuinely low-frequency configuration (settings) or for panes that are truly mutually exclusive alternates the user consciously switches between, not as a way to chop up one coherent capability into bite-size pieces.
- Group frequently paired tools/commands so they are spatially adjacent and simultaneously visible, put high-frequency actions on toolbars/palettes, reserve menus for the infrequent stuff, because menu contents are invisible until opened and every extra click against a shared control is repeated friction (and a repetitive-strain risk at volume).
- Add signposts (persistent nav bar, menu bar, toolbar, distinguishable window chrome) and overviews (breadcrumbs, thumbnail maps, annotated scrollbars) to any interface with more than a couple of navigable places or any content space long/large enough to get lost in.
- Audit every control for mapping: does its position/shape suggest what it acts on, and does its label match how the persona actually thinks about the underlying concept (recent/oldest, not ascending/descending)? Fix mapping problems early, they are cheap to fix and disproportionately improve perceived quality.
- Default the represented model of any storage/browsing feature to a flat, monocline structure (search, tags, flat lists) even if the backing implementation is a deep hierarchy for performance reasons. Give users tools that bring the right data to them instead of making them walk a tree.
- When adapting a familiar pre-digital object (calendars, address books, forms) to a digital product, explicitly ask which of its constraints were artifacts of paper/hardware and no longer apply on screen, then redesign around the removed constraint rather than reproducing the old shape out of habit.
- Kill needless modal interruptions: no error dialogs that just restate a problem the app should have handled, no confirmation dialogs unless there is a genuinely irreversible, high-stakes action and even then pair it with real information and a working Undo instead.
- Let users edit values directly wherever those values are displayed, do not force a detour to a separate edit screen or an explicit "request to edit" step.
- Never make users reenter settings they already configured, never force them to fill fields they've chosen to skip, never make them resize or reposition windows the app could size/place correctly itself, and design so user actions cannot result in unrecoverable errors in the first place.

## Watch out for

- Don't assume all excise is bad in the abstract, first check it against the specific persona's goals and posture. Configuring shared screen space is excise for most users but a goal-directed feature for a persona who genuinely needs to compare two apps side by side.
- Watch for tabs used as a bandage over "too many controls," Cooper is explicit that chopping one capability into tabbed fragments increases excise and hurts users' overall understanding, it does not make the capability easier.
- Zooming and panning (especially combined) are the hardest navigation types for most users because people aren't naturally wired for unconstrained movement through 2D/3D space, reserve them for genuinely spatial data and make them discretionary, not required to complete a task.
- Logical zoom (zooming in to reveal more attribute detail rather than more spatial detail) works well for spatially-grounded data like maps, but is usually the wrong idiom for abstract data spaces, an adjacent detail pane usually serves those better.
- Skeuomorphism is a trap that's easy to justify as "user friendliness." It is valid only while the mechanical metaphor is teaching a genuinely equivalent function, once users have learned the basics, keeping the metaphor going is pure overhead (Cooper cites Apple's iOS 4 to 6 skeuomorphism, corrected in iOS 7, as the canonical industry example).
- Don't let a persistent signpost or toolbar become removable via an accidental one-click "ejector seat" control, if a persistent object can disappear, make that action deliberate and hard to trigger by accident, and give the user an obvious way to bring it back.
- Adapting an old model to digital doesn't have to preserve its exact shape, but a partial adaptation that keeps the old model's core limitation while adding new complexity is worse than doing nothing (the iOS 7 calendar example: continuous scrolling but still hard month breaks and no drag-select). If you change a familiar format, make sure the change is significantly better, not just different.
