# Designing the Product: Framework and Refinement

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 5.

## In one line

Design moves top down, from a loose, low fidelity Design Framework (structure, flow, groupings, form) toward a fully detailed Refinement, and both stages lean on named principles, key path and validation scenarios, and late formative usability testing rather than early pixel work.

## Core ideas

- Design Framework before detail. Before touching widgets, colors, or exact layout, settle the big structural questions: what screens or views exist, how they relate, how work flows between them. This is like deciding a house's room layout before picking doorknobs. Jumping to detail too early locks in structure that has not been validated yet.
- Framework has three (sometimes four) parallel tracks: an interaction framework (behavior and screen structure), a visual design framework (look, feel, brand expression), and, when hardware is involved, an industrial design framework, plus a service design framework when the product spans multiple touchpoints. Form and behavior must be designed together, neither is subordinate to the other.
- Stay low fidelity on purpose. Render early framework ideas as rough sketches (Cooper's studio calls this the "rectangles phase") rather than polished mockups. Rough, sketch-like renderings invite critique and revision, polished ones make people feel a decision is already final and defend it instead of improving it. Sketchy visuals signal "this is still work in progress" and keep the conversation on fundamentals: does this serve the persona's goals.
- Revision is expected, not a failure. The purpose of representing a design at each stage is to sharpen everyone's understanding, including the designer's. Spend only enough effort to provoke real discussion, not enough to make change painful.
- Requirements become concrete design elements in two forms: data elements (the objects the product represents and manipulates, e.g. photos, contacts, orders, along with their key attributes and their containment or association relationships) and functional elements (the tools and operations that act on those objects). A single requirement often maps to several distinct functional elements, so don't assume a one to one mapping.
- Every element must trace back to a requirement, a scenario, or a business goal. If you can't point to why an element exists, it is probably scope creep or unexamined habit.
- Grouping and hierarchy follow flow, not convenience. Cluster elements by how personas actually use them together: adjacency for things compared or used together, sequential order for steps in a process, big screen space for what needs it. Mental models of the persona should shape the grouping, not the engineering data model.
- Two ways into the framework process: verbal thinkers often do better going scenario first, then work out groupings, then sketch. Visual thinkers often do better sketching first, then writing key path scenarios, then checking whether the groupings hold up. Neither order is more correct, pick whichever surfaces problems faster for the people involved.
- Key path scenarios are the frequent, everyday paths through the interaction framework (checking mail, not configuring a mail server). They are task oriented rather than pure goal narrative, and they get progressively more detailed as the framework itself gets more detailed. Storyboards (sketch sequences plus narrative) are the standard way to walk these paths and check coherence and flow before commit.
- Validate deliberately by severity, in a fixed order: alternative scenarios (less common branches off the key path, including secondary persona variations), necessary use scenarios (infrequent but required actions such as purging data or upgrading, which need in-context guidance precisely because users forget how to do them), and edge cases (atypical situations the product must still survive but that should never drive the design). Developers naturally over-focus on edge cases because that is where bugs live, designers must resist that pull: daily use and necessary use determine whether the product succeeds, edge case handling is a robustness backstop, not the design's purpose.
- The visual design framework runs its own top-down funnel: experience attributes (3 to 5 adjectives capturing tone, voice, brand promise) come first, then visual language studies (abstract explorations of color, type, materiality, decoupled from actual screens so stakeholders react to tone, not to interaction choices), then applying the chosen style to a real screen archetype once the interaction framework has started to stabilize.
- Unlike interaction design (which converges toward one right behavioral structure per requirement), visual design tolerates several simultaneously valid visual directions. Present a genuine spread, commonly by optimizing different directions for different experience attributes, so stakeholders choose based on brand fit rather than personal taste. Deliberately including one or two intentionally extreme directions makes the more moderate options easier to evaluate by contrast.
- Industrial design (when hardware is in scope) follows the same top-down shape: agree on form factor and input method jointly with interaction designers first (screen size, buttons, touch/voice/motion input), then build rough physical prototypes, then run form language studies (materials, finish, dimensionality). Get mechanical and electrical engineers involved early, since something as small as a hinge or a battery choice can reshape the whole feasible design space.
- Service design (when the product spans multiple touchpoints or channels) maps customer journeys first (narrative walk of a persona through the whole service, echoing context scenarios), then a service blueprint (the full picture of front stage touchpoints and back stage delivery processes, often drawn as a swimlane diagram with a line of visibility separating what the customer sees from what they don't), then experience prototypes illustrating continuity across touchpoints. Start from the customer's path through personas, not from the org chart, because starting internally tends to miss touchpoints customers actually hit.
- Refinement repeats the same step sequence as Framework, just at increasing depth, funneling sketches into full pixel level screens. Form factor and input method rarely get revisited here unless a real manufacturing or cost problem forces it. Developers should be closely involved throughout Refinement, since it's the phase where concept must survive contact with what can actually be built.
- The deliverable at the end of Refinement is often a Form and Behavior Specification: detailed screen renderings with callouts a developer can code from, plus storyboards showing behavior over time. Prototypes (HTML, etc.) can supplement this but should not replace it, a working prototype rarely communicates the underlying rationale and principles a developer needs to extend the design correctly later. Whatever the deliverable, the design team must stay engaged through implementation, the vision degrades in translation without vigilance.
- Usability testing evaluates, it does not generate ideas. It smooths and validates a design that already has a considered foundation, it will never manufacture the compelling idea that makes a product good. Time is better spent on user research before ideation and on considered design after it, than on testing a design built without that foundation.
- Usability testing is not user research. Research (interviews, task analysis, ethnography) has to happen before ideation to ground personas and scenarios. Testing and feedback happen after a design exists, to check it. Conflating these into one activity blurs what each is actually good for.
- Summative versus formative evaluation (concept from Jakob Nielsen). Summative evaluations judge a finished product (comparisons, pre-redesign audits, support/return root cause analysis), typically formal, quantitative, third party run. Formative evaluations run during design, especially during Refinement, and are quick, qualitative, and meant to feed changes back into the design while changes are still cheap. Running only summative tests late, near ship, as a quality gate is a trap: by then there's no runway left to act on what you learn.
- Usability testing is strong at revealing first-time-use problems (labeling, discoverability, initial task completion) and weak at revealing how a design holds up for the perpetual intermediate user on their 50th use. A diary study (participants log their own real usage over time) is the tool for that longer horizon question, a single test session cannot answer it.

## Named patterns and principles

- **Design Framework**: the phase where interaction, visual, and (if relevant) industrial and service structure gets defined at a high, low-detail level before granular design. Use it whenever a product's overall shape isn't settled yet, it exists so structural mistakes get caught while they're still cheap to fix.
- **Pretend the product is human**: when deciding on interaction details and functional groupings, ask what a polite, considerate human assistant would do in this moment, would offer, would avoid making you repeat. Use it while shaping behaviors and elements, not just at the concept stage. It works because people already have rich, effortless intuitions about considerate human behavior, and borrowing those intuitions produces more humane software than reasoning from abstract UI conventions alone.
- **Rectangles phase**: the practice of sketching the interaction framework as bare labeled rectangles for panes, containers, and controls, with arrows for flow, before any real layout or widget design. Use it at the start of Step 4 sketching. It works by keeping the group's attention on structure and relationships instead of premature visual commitment.
- **Key path scenarios**: detailed, task oriented walkthroughs of the interface following the persona's most frequent daily paths, expressed in the vocabulary of the actual interaction framework (not abstract goals). Use them to pressure test and iteratively detail the framework as design deepens. They work because frequent paths are where design quality is felt every day, they deserve the most design attention.
- **Validation scenarios (alternative, necessary use, edge case)**: a three tier, priority ordered set of what-if checks run against a design once the key path is solid. Alternative scenarios are less traveled branches off the main path, necessary use scenarios are rare but mandatory actions that need extra in-context guidance because users forget them, edge cases are atypical situations the product must survive but that should get minimal interface real estate. Use this order (alternative, then necessary use, then edge case) to allocate design effort by actual daily impact, not by where bugs are likely to hide.
- **Experience attributes**: 3 to 5 adjectives (refined through stakeholder review, competitive comparison, and disambiguation of double meanings) that define the tone and brand promise the visual design should express. Use them at the start of visual design framework work, before any visual language studies, so subsequent visual choices have an agreed vocabulary instead of resting on individual taste.
- **Visual language studies**: abstract, interaction-independent explorations of color, type, material, and dimensionality, tied back to experience attributes. Use them to get stakeholder reaction to tone and brand fit without the distraction of concrete interaction decisions. They work because separating "how it feels" from "how it behaves" lets each get evaluated on its own terms.
- **"Never show a design approach you're unhappy with, stakeholders just might like it."** Applies when curating options for a stakeholder review: only present directions the team could live with shipping, because presenting a deliberately weak option as a strawman risks it being the one chosen.
- **"There is only one user experience: form and behavior must be designed in concert."** Applies whenever industrial and interaction design intersect (custom hardware, physical form factor). Neither track can be finalized in isolation, function should lead, but cost and fabrication constraints will always push back on it.
- **Summative vs. formative evaluation** (Nielsen's distinction): summative tests judge a finished product and are typically quantitative and third party led, formative tests run during the design process (best in Refinement) to catch and fix problems while change is still cheap. Use formative testing as your default during active design, reserve summative testing for product comparison, redesign triage, or post launch diagnosis, not as a last minute quality gate.
- **Diary study**: participants log real interactions with the product over an extended period. Use it when you need insight into intermediate or expert, repeated use rather than first-time use, which one-off usability sessions cannot reveal.

## How to apply

- When starting a new product or major feature, do not sketch pixel-level screens first. Establish form factor, posture, and primary input method, then catalog data and functional elements traced to specific requirements, then group them by flow and mental model, then sketch structure as labeled boxes, then write a key path scenario, then stress test with validation scenarios in the alternative, necessary use, edge case order.
- When several teams (interaction, visual, industrial, service) are involved, run their framework tracks in parallel but keep them synced, form and behavior decisions constrain each other and should not be finalized independently.
- Keep early design artifacts visibly rough (sketches, wireframe-level fidelity) as long as the structure is still open to real change. Only invest in polished, pixel-accurate renderings once a decision is meant to be treated as settled.
- When proposing functional elements, explicitly ask: does this best accomplish the persona's goal, does it fit the product's stated design principles, does it fit cost/tech constraints, does it differentiate appropriately, does it fit the rest of the requirements. Use this as a checklist before locking in a solution.
- Before defaulting to a novel interaction, check for an existing pattern or principle that solves the same problem. Only invent a new solution when there's a real, articulable reason the standard approach won't serve this persona or context.
- When choosing between multiple valid solutions to a requirement, use "what would a considerate, helpful human do here" as a practical tiebreaker for tone and behavior, not just for wording.
- Schedule usability testing to land late enough that there's real design substance to react to, but early enough that findings can still change the implementation. Treat it as a phase inside Refinement, not a final gate before ship.
- Before running any usability test, confirm you can actually measure what you're testing, that recruiting matches your personas, that a moderator not previously on the project will run it to reduce bias, and that you have resources budgeted to actually fix what you find. A test whose findings can't be acted on isn't worth running.
- Keep designers actively involved in usability studies: shaping the research questions, using personas to define recruiting criteria, using scenarios to build test tasks, observing sessions directly, and joining the analysis. Do not treat testing as something to fully outsource and read about later.
- If the goal is understanding long-term or expert usage rather than first-time learnability, plan a diary study or equivalent longitudinal method instead of (or alongside) a standard usability test.

## Watch out for

- Do not let engineering's natural focus on edge cases (where bugs concentrate) pull design attention away from key path and necessary use scenarios, which is where user-perceived success or failure actually lives.
- Do not treat usability testing as a substitute for upfront user research or for actual design thinking. It reveals problems in an existing design, it does not generate the design.
- Do not run summative, quality-assurance-style evaluations as your only or primary check, and especially not late in development as a gate. By then there is rarely enough runway left to make meaningful changes from what you learn.
- Do not present a design direction to stakeholders that the team would be unhappy to have chosen, someone will like it.
- Do not skip straight to detailed, high-fidelity mockups when the underlying structure (screens, flows, groupings) is still unsettled, it discourages the team from abandoning a flawed structure once real effort is sunk into its surface.
- Do not assume a brand's existing marketing style guide translates directly into an interactive product's visual language, it usually needs real translation work, and it rarely accounts for accessibility needs like contrast for outdoor/bright-light use or larger type for sight-impaired personas.
- Do not conflate research activities (interviews, task analysis, participatory design) with usability testing, they serve different points in the process and blurring them wastes both.
