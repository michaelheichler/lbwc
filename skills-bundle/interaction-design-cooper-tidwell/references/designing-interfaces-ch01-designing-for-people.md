# Designing for People

Source: Designing Interfaces: Patterns for Effective Interaction Design, 3rd Edition (Jenifer Tidwell, Charles Brewer, Aynne Valencia, 2020), Chapter 1.

## In one line

Good interface design starts before any screen is drawn, with a real understanding of who the user is, what they are actually trying to accomplish, and how people generically think and behave, not with picking widgets.

## Core ideas

- **You are not the user.** Never substitute your own assumptions, preferences, or mental model for the target audience's. Design decisions need to be grounded in real people, not the designer's intuition.
- **Every interaction is a conversation.** The user and the software exchange requests, commands, and responses back and forth. The user needs continuous feedback that confirms the system heard them, is working, and is progressing toward their goal. Design the "conversational manners" of the interface. Be understandable, signal when you're listening, signal when you're responding, and anticipate the next likely thing the user needs.
- **Match content, vocabulary, and complexity to the audience.** Just as a real conversation partner adjusts tone and vocabulary to the listener, an interface should adjust its terminology, its assumed domain knowledge, and its interaction sophistication to match the audience. Getting this wrong makes users feel patronized, lost, or disengaged.
- **Choose the right amount of freedom.** Decide where your product sits between a tightly scripted wizard (only Next/Previous/Cancel, efficient but limiting) and an open floorplan like a spreadsheet (huge surface of features, satisfying for skilled self-directed users but overwhelming otherwise). Too little freedom feels trapping, too much causes paralysis.
- **Design for the real skill level of your users, honestly.** Ask whether most users will become experts (daily use, tolerate a learning curve, but grow intolerant of friction over time), stay occasional or casual (kiosks, one-off purchase flows, ATMs, need heavy guidance and low complexity), or land in the large middle ground (email clients, PowerPoint, social apps) that must onboard easily while still rewarding frequent use. Expert-oriented tools optimize for efficiency over learnability. Novice-oriented tools optimize for guidance over power.
- **The interface is only a means to the user's end.** Nobody's goal is "fill out a form", they want to buy something, renew a license, or finish a transaction. Treat every UI element as instrumental to a deeper goal, never as an end in itself.
- **Ask "why" repeatedly (the five whys, applied to user goals).** Push past the stated feature request to the underlying motive, then push again. This surfaces the actual problem so you can potentially eliminate steps entirely, not just polish them. Removing a form is usually a better outcome than perfecting it.
- **Users have goals that can be classified**: finding something, learning, transacting, monitoring or controlling, creating, conversing with others, being entertained. Different design patterns support each. A single site may serve multiple goal types for the same user in different visits or moments, and users can switch goals mid-session (a shopper gets absorbed in content, an entertainment seeker starts a purchase).
- **Design research vs. marketing research are related but distinct.** Design research wants to understand the people using the software (their tasks and behavior). Marketing research wants to understand the people buying it (demographics, psychographics). Interviews and surveys serve both but for different questions.
- **Self-reported data is biased.** Users can't always articulate what they need, and observation of behavior often reveals more than what people say about themselves. Formal, careful methods correct for this bias, but informal contact beats skipping research entirely.
- **Research methods each trade off depth vs. scale**: interviews and on-site observation give deep qualitative insight and flexibility but small sample sizes, case studies give longitudinal or extreme-user depth, surveys give statistically valid breadth but need careful question design and no direct human context, personas are not a data-gathering method but a synthesis tool to keep the whole team focused on a concrete stand-in for each user segment while designing.
- **People behave predictably even though individuals are unique.** The chapter treats a set of general human cognition and behavior patterns as reliable enough to design around, distinct from the book's usual prescriptive UI patterns because these describe human behavior, not interface structure.

## Named patterns and principles

**Safe Exploration**: Users need to feel free to try something, back out, and try something else without cost or stress. What it is: an environment where mistakes are cheap or reversible. When to use: everywhere, especially first-time use and unfamiliar features. Why it works: fear of "dire consequences" (even mild annoyance like a lost form or a jarring pop-up) suppresses learning and exploration. Safety produces more positive, more thorough engagement. How-to: support reliable Back/Undo behavior, avoid surprise pop-ups or state changes, pair with Multilevel Undo.

**Instant Gratification**: Users want a visible win within the first seconds of use. What it is: designing the first action to be quick and rewarding. When to use: onboarding and first-run experience. Why it works: an early success experience builds confidence in the tool and in the user's own ability, increasing the odds they keep going even if things get harder later. How-to: predict the user's likely first action and make it obvious and frictionless, put a clear call to action up front, delay registration, ads, and long instructions until after value has been delivered.

**Satisficing**: Users scan quickly and pick the first option that looks like it might work, rather than exhaustively evaluating all choices (Herbert Simon, 1957: "satisfying" plus "sufficing"). When to use: assume this happens on every screen, always. Why it works: thoroughly evaluating a complex interface is real mental work, so choosing "good enough" fast is actually rational behavior. How-to: put clear calls to action, write short plain labels that match users' first guess at meaning, use visual layout (not just text) to communicate structure since people parse form and color before reading, make backing out of a wrong choice cheap, remember that visual complexity itself pushes non-experts into satisficing and old-habit paths even when a better path exists.

**Changes in Midstream**: Users often change their goal partway through a task, sidetracked or deliberately. What it is: recognizing that the user's intent inside a session is not fixed. When to use: whenever a flow could plausibly lead somewhere else the user might want to go. Why it works: locking users into a narrow, connectionless flow fights against real behavior. How-to: keep escape routes and links available unless there's a deliberate reason not to (see Wizard, Modal Panel), and support reentrance, letting a user pause a task and resume later without losing state (e.g. persist half-filled forms).

**Deferred Choices**: Users want to skip nonessential questions now and answer them later, or never. What it is: separating must-answer-now fields from can-answer-later fields. When to use: registration flows, project setup, any form with optional or premature questions. Why it works: forcing decisions before the user has enough information or motivation causes drop-off. Instant gratification demands minimizing upfront friction. How-to: mark required vs optional clearly and keep required list short, hide the long list of secondary options, use Good Defaults for deferred fields, let users return to unanswered fields later from an obvious place, consider deferring registration itself until after the user has gotten value (e.g. completed a purchase) from the product.

**Incremental Construction**: Creative work happens in small nonlinear steps (build a bit, look at it, fix it, build more), not start-to-finish in order. What it is: the natural workflow of builder-style tools (editors, IDEs, paint programs). When to use: any tool where users create or assemble something. Why it works: matches how people actually think and create. Fast feedback loops enable a state of flow (Csikszentmihalyi), while slow feedback breaks concentration and disengages the user. How-to: support building in small pieces, keep the interface responsive to quick changes and saves, show live feedback on the whole artifact continuously, minimize compile or preview delay to near zero.

**Habituation**: Frequently repeated actions become reflexive muscle memory (Ctrl-S, Back button, standard gestures) and no longer require conscious thought. What it is: automatized user behavior built from repetition. When to use: whenever a gesture, shortcut, or interaction pattern exists elsewhere in the platform or in your own app. Why it works: it makes expert use fast and supports flow, but the same reflex fires in contexts where it does not apply or is destructive, catching users off guard. How-to: keep gestures and shortcuts consistent both across the platform (use standard conventions) and within your own app (never let the same gesture mean different things in different modes), be wary that confirmation dialogs stop protecting users once clicking OK/Return itself becomes habitual.

**Microbreaks**: Users often have a few idle minutes (waiting in line, on a train) and want something quick, easy, and low-commitment to fill the time. What it is: designing for short, interruptible sessions. When to use: mobile apps, feeds, casual games, anything likely to be opened for two minutes at a time. Why it works: this is the behavioral engine behind social media and casual gaming success. How-to: make launch instant (no complicated setup, no slow load, persist prior sign-in), surface freshest content immediately for feeds, restore previous state without asking for games, videos, or books (supports reentrance), and support quick triage (star, delete, short reply) for housekeeping-type apps like email.

**Spatial Memory**: People often find things by remembering where they are, not what they're called. What it is: reliance on location and layout, not labels, for recall. When to use: dialog buttons, toolbars, menu structure, desktop-style workspaces, anything with a fixed screen home. Why it works: spatial recall is strong and largely automatic. Disrupting a remembered layout costs real usability even if the new layout is "better" on paper. How-to: keep dialog button placement and menu order consistent and predictable, let users self-arrange objects (e.g. a desktop) and never rearrange it for them uninvited, be cautious with dynamic/responsive UI changes to existing controls, and note that first and last items in a list are the most noticed and remembered, so put the important items at the ends, not the middle.

**Prospective Memory**: People plan to do something later and plant an external reminder to compensate for imperfect memory (a book by the front door, an email left open, a calendar alarm). What it is: designing around the user's need for self-made memory aids. When to use: any system where users park unfinished work or intentions. Why it works: people build ad hoc reminder systems out of whatever flexible, non-judgmental storage is available. Systems that are too "smart" (auto-closing idle windows, auto-organizing files) destroy those improvised reminders. How-to: give flexible, hands-off storage (arbitrary folder names, freeform notes, do-not-auto-clean idle items), retain state of half-finished work (ties to Deferred Choices), remember recently used objects or documents, and consider aggregating reminders across sources (email, docs, calendar) into one place.

**Streamlined Repetition**: Users doing the same operation many times want it reduced to as few actions as possible. What it is: batching or automating repeated operations. When to use: any workflow involving multiple similar repetitions (find/replace, batch image edits, repeated shell commands). Why it works: repetitive manual work is tedious and error-prone. One gesture replacing many is a large, well-liked efficiency win. How-to: offer batch operations (Replace All), recordable action sequences (see Macros, Chapter 8), scripting or command recall, copy-paste, shortcuts, and bookmarks. Watch users directly since they often don't notice their own repetitive patterns well enough to report them.

**Keyboard Only**: Some users cannot or prefer not to use a mouse (motor impairment, efficiency preference, screen-reader or assistive tech users) and need full functionality via keyboard alone. What it is: a requirement that no operation is mouse-exclusive. When to use: always as a baseline, and especially critical for data-entry-heavy applications where speed matters. Why it works: excluding keyboard-only users blocks them entirely, and even mouse-preferring power users benefit from faster keyboard paths. How-to: define standard shortcuts, accelerators, and mnemonics per platform conventions, support arrow-key list selection with modifiers, support Tab/Shift-Tab focus traversal, let standard controls accept keyboard input (arrows, Return, space), and define a clear default button that Return triggers on forms and dialogs.

**Social Media, Social Proof, and Collaboration**: People are influenced by what peers do and say, and seek belonging and approval. What it is: designing social signals and collaborative structures into the product. When to use: reviews and ratings, anything shareable, communities, multi-person work (documents, discussions, status tracking). Why it works: social proof measurably drives action (watch, buy, join, share) and social participation or recognition is intrinsically rewarding, which is why it underlies most successful software today. How-to: support user-generated reviews and comments with reviewer reputation, treat user-created content (posts, images, checkins) as shareable "social objects" people can gather around and discuss, and build collaboration features (threads, reviews, conferencing, status tracking) for cross-time, cross-space teamwork. Related to Help Systems (community support as part of a help system).

## How to apply

- Before any screen design, define who the audience is, what they're trying to accomplish, their domain vocabulary, their skill level, and their attitude toward the subject. Do this even informally rather than skipping it.
- For every requested feature, ask "why" repeatedly until you reach the user's real underlying goal, then check whether the feature (or even the whole step, like a form) can be eliminated rather than merely refined.
- Place the product deliberately on the freedom spectrum (wizard-tight vs. open-floorplan) based on actual user skill and motivation to learn, not designer preference.
- Design the first few seconds of first use for a fast, obvious win.
- Make backing out of any action cheap and predictable (reliable Back/Undo) so users feel safe exploring.
- Write short, plain labels users can correctly guess on first glance, and lean on layout and color, not just text, to convey structure.
- Separate must-answer-now fields from can-defer-later fields on any form, and persist partial input.
- For creative or builder tools, keep feedback loops (preview, compile, save) as close to instant as possible to protect flow state.
- Keep gestures, shortcuts, button placement, and menu order consistent both within the app and against platform norms.
- Support reentrance everywhere plausible: let users pause and resume without losing state or having to redo work.
- For mobile or casual-use surfaces, optimize for instant launch and remembered state, since users may arrive with only a couple of idle minutes.
- Never require the mouse for any function. Ensure full keyboard operability, especially in data-entry-heavy tools.
- Where a social or community layer would add trust or engagement (reviews, sharing, collaboration), design it in deliberately rather than bolting it on.
- Choose research methods to fit the question and budget: informal interviews and observation for qualitative understanding, surveys for statistically valid breadth, case studies for depth or longitudinal insight, personas to keep the team aligned once data exists. Some research beats none.

## Watch out for

- Don't design from your own assumptions as a stand-in for the user ("you are not the user").
- Don't hide the first useful action behind registration, long instructions, ads, or slow loading screens, it kills instant gratification and increases drop-off.
- Don't rearrange existing controls, menus, or layout "helpfully" without a strong reason. It breaks users' spatial memory and habituation even when the new arrangement is objectively better.
- Don't let the same gesture behave differently in different modes within your own app. Habituated users will trigger the wrong action and may not notice until damage is done.
- Don't rely on confirmation dialogs as a safety net once clicking through them becomes habitual, by that point users click OK reflexively and the dialog stops protecting anything.
- Don't let a system "helpfully" auto-clean, auto-close, or auto-organize items a user left in place. Those artifacts may be intentional prospective-memory reminders, not clutter.
- Don't ask for too many upfront choices or require answers the user isn't ready to give yet. Defer what can be deferred.
- Don't confuse design research (understanding users of the software) with marketing research (understanding buyers of the software), they answer different questions even though the methods overlap.
- Don't trust self-reported user statements at face value. People often can't articulate their real needs, and biased or haphazard research can be worse than no research if it creates false confidence.
- Don't model your audience as a single faceless "the user" with one linear goal, real users switch goals mid-session and bring soft factors (expectations, beliefs, social context) that shape what actually works.
