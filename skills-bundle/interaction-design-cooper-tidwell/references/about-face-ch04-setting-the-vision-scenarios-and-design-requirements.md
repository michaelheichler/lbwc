# Setting the Vision: Scenarios and Design Requirements

Source: About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design, 4th Edition (Cooper, Reimann, Cronin, Noessel, 2014), Chapter 4.

## In one line

Bridge the gap between user research and a shippable design by using persona-driven narrative (scenarios) to discover what the product must do (design requirements) before anyone starts deciding how it looks or behaves.

## Core ideas

**The research-design gap.** Teams that do good research or good brainstorming still stall when it is time to make real design decisions, because neither activity by itself produces an organizing principle that connects user needs to a coherent set of features. Something has to translate insight into direction. That connective tissue is narrative built around personas.

**Why narrative works as a design tool.** Telling a story about a specific persona using the product forces more creative and more empathetic thinking than imagining a screen layout in isolation. Stories are also inherently social, so they are easy to share with teammates and stakeholders and easier for everyone to evaluate than an abstract feature list. Interaction design is the design of behavior over time, and narrative is naturally suited to representing behavior over time.

**Keep early scenarios rough, like storyboards.** Interaction narratives function like film storyboards, they need a plot and they need to stay brief. Over-detailing early scenarios wastes effort and creates false attachment to a specific solution just because it took work to draw, before the underlying concept has been validated. Stay at "plot point" fidelity while exploring, and save polished renderings for once a design framework exists.

**Scenarios are not use cases, and not user stories.** Use cases are exhaustive, transactional descriptions of system responses to user actions. They treat every possible interaction as equally likely and important, which is a software-engineering habit, not an interaction-design one. They are useful late, for checking completeness and catching edge cases, not for shaping the design's priorities. User stories (as used in agile) are short, task-level requirement statements ("As a user, I want to log in..."), not narratives, and they do not capture the user's overall flow or end goal. Persona-based scenarios sit at a broader, more strategic level, closer to what agile calls an epic: a cluster of interactions organized around a user goal, described in terms of user behavior rather than a checklist of functions.

**Why Carroll's scenario-based design needed an upgrade.** Carroll's HCI research established the core value of scenarios: concrete but flexible, they invite "what if" thinking without prematurely locking in a solution. But his actors are abstract, role-based stand-ins (for example "Accountant"), too thin to build real empathy or infer specific behavior from. And his scenarios jump straight to tasks without first grounding those tasks in the user's goals and motivations, defining "goal" circularly as "finishing the task." Swapping in fully-realized personas fixes both problems. Personas carry real motivations, so the scenario can explore what tasks and priorities should be, not just narrate existing ones, and they broaden what a scenario can be used for, up to defining the product itself.

**Persona-based scenarios.** These are concise narratives of one or more personas using the (future) product to reach specific goals, told from the persona's point of view (people and behavior first, technology and business logic second). Content is drawn directly from research and modeling work. Constructing them is a kind of role-play or improvisation, typically done at a whiteboard, and the resulting scenarios later inform detailed visual and interaction design, and get used throughout the project to sanity-check design decisions.

**Three types of scenarios, in order of increasing specificity.** Context scenarios come first and are used before any interface sketching to explore, at a high level, how the product should serve persona needs. Once a Design Framework exists, the context scenario evolves into a key path scenario that names the actual interaction vocabulary and covers the most important user paths, refined iteratively as detail accumulates. Validation scenarios run throughout the process as lighter-weight "what if" stress tests of a proposed design against edge situations. (Key path and validation scenarios are covered in Chapter 5.)

**Define the what before the how.** Deciding what the product needs to do (information, capabilities) must be settled and agreed before anyone designs how it looks, behaves, or feels. Skipping straight to a rendered solution removes any objective standard for judging it, so disagreements degrade into "I like it" versus "I don't," which stakeholders cannot resolve on their own. Other creative fields separate this the same way: a graphic novelist plots a story before inking a page.

**Requirements are needs, not features.** Industry usage often conflates "requirement" with "feature" or "function." Treat design requirements instead as human and business needs the product must satisfy. Features come later, once needs are established, and the next chapter shows how requirements translate into the Design Framework.

**Requirements are not the same as an MRD/PRD spec list.** Marketing or product requirements documents try to describe the what, but they typically have three failure modes. They are often written with little or no real user research behind them, so a coherent-looking document can still describe a product nobody wants. They frequently smuggle in the how by specifying UI mechanics ("there should be a menu containing...") which forecloses better solutions before the problem is even explored. And a bare list of requirements gives stakeholders and developers nothing to visualize, so it is hard for anyone to make decisions from it. Example: a data-analytics tool spec that jumps to "should produce reports" locks in a familiar but weak solution. Scenario-driven analysis might instead reveal the real need is early exception detection and trend visibility, better served by live monitoring than static reports.

**Requirements-first is a strategic move, not paperwork.** Separating the problem (needs) from the solution (implementation) keeps the product's definition safe even when a chosen technology runs into trouble, because the underlying needs did not change. It also lets designers and technologists jointly find the best feasible solution instead of a solution baked in prematurely, and it lets the team plan a longer technology roadmap aimed at serving the same needs ever better over time.

**Requirements come from more than personas and scenarios.** Persona and scenario analysis is the primary source, but business needs, technical constraints, and legal constraints (typically surfaced through stakeholder interviews) round out the full requirements picture.

**The Requirements Definition process has five steps**, cycled iteratively rather than run once straight through (expect to loop steps 3 through 5 multiple times until requirements settle):
1. Create problem and vision statements
2. Explore and brainstorm
3. Identify persona expectations
4. Construct context scenarios
5. Identify design requirements

This phase (the "what") precedes the Framework Definition phase (the "how"), covered in the next chapter.

**Step 1, problem and vision statements.** The problem statement is a concise account of what needs to change, for the persona and for the business, and ideally ties the two together causally (weak user tooling costing market share, for example). The vision statement inverts it: lead with what the persona needs, then show how meeting that need also serves the business goal. Framing things this way builds stakeholder buy-in because it makes the business case for user-centered work explicit, rather than assuming stakeholders will take it on faith. This step matters most for redesigns and for new-to-the-world products entering unfamiliar markets, where the team most needs an explicit shared mandate.

**Step 2, explore and brainstorm.** After weeks or months of research, the team already carries unspoken assumptions about what the solution will look like. Brainstorm early specifically to get those preconceptions out of people's heads and onto paper, so they are not driving the "objective" scenario work that follows unexamined. A side benefit: brainstorming shifts the team's mindset from analysis to invention, which is a different mental mode. Keep exploration unconstrained and non-judgmental, capture even the wacky ideas for later, and use a few of the more provocative concepts as a probe with stakeholders to calibrate how much creative risk they will actually tolerate. Time-box this step (hours to a couple of days), stop once ideas start repeating.

**Step 3, identify persona expectations.** A person's mental model, their internal, largely subconscious sense of how something works, drives what they expect from a product. The design's represented model (how the interface presents and behaves) should match the user's mental model, not the implementation model (how the system is actually built internally). For each primary and secondary persona, capture attitudes and background factors shaping expectations, general expectations and desires about the experience, expected behaviors, and how the persona conceptually organizes the basic data objects involved (for example, in email, "messages" and "people"). Mine interview data for what subjects mention first, which verbs and nouns they use, and which steps or objects they never mention at all (a sign those things are not load-bearing in their mental model).

**Step 4, construct context scenarios.** This is where actual design begins. A context scenario narrates a persona using the future product in their most typical way, covering environmental and organizational context, and it should stay broad and shallow, focused on high-level user actions and outcomes rather than interface mechanics. It should describe an aspirational, Goal-Directed future, not today's product behavior, and treat the interior of the system as a black box for now. Typical questions it should answer: where and for how long is the product used, is the user interrupted often, is the device shared, what other products are involved, what is the persona actually trying to accomplish, how much complexity can the persona's skill and usage frequency tolerate. Multiple context scenarios are normal, especially with multiple primary personas or one persona operating in more than one distinct context. Keep it entirely textual at this stage, form and interface come later.

**Step 5, identify design requirements.** Analyze the finished context scenario to extract needs, framed as objects, actions, and contexts (for example, "call [action] a person [object] directly from an appointment [context]"). If that combined format is awkward, split requirements into three categories: data requirements (the objects and their attributes the system must represent, such as accounts, people, messages, and fields like status or date), functional requirements (the operations performable on those objects, which typically become interface controls, plus the places those objects need to be displayed), and contextual requirements (dependencies between objects, like needing a running total visible while selecting items to purchase, plus physical-environment and persona-skill considerations). Beyond these, capture business requirements (stakeholder priorities, timelines, budget, legal and regulatory constraints, pricing, business model), brand and experience requirements (what associations you want users to form with the product or company), technical requirements (weight, size, form factor, display, power, platform), and customer and partner requirements (install, maintenance, configuration, support, licensing). The output of all five steps is a rough but creative picture of how the product serves user goals plus a distilled requirements list, and this list also functions as an agreed scope of work. Any requirement added later is a scope change, not free.

## Named patterns and principles

- **Define what the product will do before you design how the product will do it.** Cooper's core sequencing rule for this phase. Separates the needs question from the solution question so the team has an objective way to evaluate any proposed design, instead of falling back on subjective "I like/you like" taste battles. Apply it by refusing to sketch UI until requirements are written down and agreed.

- **Context scenario.** The first and broadest of the three scenario types: a persona-perspective narrative of ideal product use, written before any interface exists, treating the system as a black box. Use it to explore what an ideal experience looks like, free of implementation thinking. Works because it keeps the team focused on goals and outcomes rather than getting anchored on mechanics too early.

- **Key path scenario.** A context scenario evolved once a Design Framework exists, now naming the actual interaction vocabulary and detailing the most significant user paths. Use it once framework-level structure exists, and refine it iteratively as detail is added. It works because it grounds the abstract goal narrative in the concrete vocabulary of the real design.

- **Validation scenario.** A lighter-weight, "what if" style scenario used throughout the process to pressure-test a design against situations outside the main path. Use it continuously during design, not just at the end, to catch gaps the key path scenario does not surface.

- **Pretend the interface is magic.** A generative technique for early context-scenario drafting: imagine the persona's goals get met by a magic system with no technical limits, then work backward toward what is actually buildable. It works because it strips away premature technical self-censorship and reveals what "ideal" really looks like from the persona's side. Products that come close to that ideal, with minimal hassle and intrusion, feel almost magical to real users even though the mechanism underneath is ordinary engineering.

- **Problem statement and vision statement.** A paired framing tool. The problem statement names what is wrong for both persona and business (often causally linked, for example poor tooling driving down satisfaction and market share). The vision statement inverts it into a design mandate that leads with user needs and shows how meeting them serves the business. Use both at the start of a project, especially redesigns or unfamiliar new markets, to build stakeholder consensus before ideation starts. Works because it makes the user-business link explicit instead of assumed.

- **Requirements Definition process (five steps).** Problem and vision statements, explore and brainstorm, identify persona expectations, construct context scenarios, identify design requirements. An iterative sequence (steps 3 to 5 loop) that turns research and personas into an agreed, testable list of needs. Use it as the standard front-half workflow before framework and detailed design begin.

- **Data, functional, and contextual requirements.** A three-way split for extracted needs: data (objects and their attributes), functional (operations on those objects, and where they must be displayed), contextual (dependencies between objects and environmental or persona-skill constraints). Use this breakdown whenever the combined "action-object-context" phrasing feels unwieldy. It works because it separates concerns cleanly enough to hand off to different specialists (information architecture, interaction, environment and accessibility) without losing the connections between them.

## How to apply

- Do not open a design tool or write interface copy until you have a written, stakeholder-agreed set of design requirements. Treat "what" and "how" as strictly sequential phases.
- Before drafting any scenario, dump every solution idea already lodged in your head (and the team's) into a time-boxed brainstorm, explicitly to clear it out of the way, not to select from it yet.
- Write persona expectations first: mental model, expected behaviors, and how the persona names and groups the core data objects. Pull it from persona docs and raw interview language, not from assumption.
- Draft context scenarios in prose, from the persona's point of view, broad and shallow, with the system's internals left as a black box. Use the magic-interface trick to surface the truly ideal experience before worrying about feasibility.
- Extract requirements from the scenario as object, action, and context triples, or split them into data, functional, and contextual buckets if that is clearer for the team.
- Separately gather business, brand and experience, technical, and customer and partner requirements from stakeholder interviews. Do not let personas be the only source.
- Treat the resulting requirements list as the agreed scope. If new requirements surface later, flag them explicitly as scope changes rather than quietly folding them in.
- Escalate scenario detail only as the design matures: context scenario when defining the product for the first time, key path scenario once a framework exists, validation scenarios continuously to stress-test.
- When facing a "requirements" document that reads like a UI spec ("add a menu with these items"), push back and re-derive the underlying need before accepting the described solution.

## Watch out for

- Treating a use case or a set of agile user stories as if it were a scenario. Use cases assume every path is equally important and skip specifying actual system behavior. User stories are task-level requirement fragments, not narratives, and neither captures the persona's overall goal or flow.
- Over-rendering early scenarios. Detailed visuals early cost time and money and create sunk-cost attachment to a solution that has not been validated yet, keep early passes at storyboard-level plot points.
- Letting an MRD/PRD stand in for real requirements work. These documents are often written with little grounding in user research, frequently smuggle interface decisions (the how) into what is supposed to be a needs statement (the what), and give stakeholders nothing concrete to visualize or evaluate.
- Confusing "requirement" with "feature." A requirement is a need. A feature is one possible way to satisfy it. Naming a feature too early forecloses better solutions.
- Skipping the requirements phase to get to a visible design faster. Without agreed requirements, there is no objective way to evaluate the resulting design, so debates degrade into unresolvable taste arguments and iteration never converges.
- Basing context scenarios on how the current product actually behaves rather than on the Goal-Directed ideal. The point of a context scenario is to imagine a better future, not to document the status quo.
